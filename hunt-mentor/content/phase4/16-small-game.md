> [!why]
> Snowshoe hare is the cheapest, longest and most forgiving hunt in Region 3, and the best classroom for your .22. It also carries one disease you must respect.

- In BC law, small game is fox, raccoon, coyote, skunk, snowshoe hare and game birds (98%).
- No species licence; your basic resident hunting licence covers small game (98%).
- Region 3 snowshoe hare: Aug 1 to Apr 30, 10 a day (98%).
- Cottontails, European rabbits and grey squirrels are "Schedule C": no licence, no closed season, no bag limit, but they live far from Heffley Creek (98%).
- Red squirrels and ground squirrels are not game for a hunter: trapping only, or private land only (95%).
- Tularemia, "rabbit fever", is rare in BC but real. Gloves, no sick animals, cook it through (95%).

## What "small game" means in BC

"SMALL GAME - includes fox, raccoon, coyote, skunk, snowshoe hare and game birds." (Synopsis page 4) (98%)

"GAME BIRD - means any grouse, partridge, quail, pheasant, ptarmigan, migratory game bird, or wild turkey." (Synopsis page 3) (98%)

- **Schedule B** animals may be killed on private land to protect property, with no licence or season; on Crown land you follow the open seasons (98%). The list includes snowshoe hare, Columbian ground squirrel, yellow bellied marmot, porcupine, striped skunk, raccoon, crows and magpies (98%).
- **Schedule C** animals have no closed season or bag limit, no edible portion rule and no report; you do not need a hunting licence for them (98%). The list: American bullfrog, green frog, snapping turtle, North American opossum, eastern cottontail, European rabbit, nutria, gray squirrels, fox squirrels, house sparrow, European starling and rock dove (98%).
- All other hunting rules still apply to Schedule B and C: hours, no shooting areas, vehicle rules, no trafficking in meat (98%). If you injure one you must kill it (98%).
- Feral pigs need a hunting licence unless on your own land, and a Compulsory Report anywhere in BC (98%).
- Red, Douglas and northern flying squirrels are furbearing animals with trapping seasons, not hunting seasons (95%).

## Snowshoe hare: identify and biology

- A hare, not a rabbit: born furred with eyes open; adults 1.2 to 1.6 kg (85%).
- Grey brown in summer, white in winter, with huge furred hind feet; moults in August to September and March to April (85%).
- On the wet southwest coast of BC, hares stay brown all year (85%).
- Home range about 6 to 10 ha, laced with packed trails called runways that show up after the first snow (85%).
- Numbers rise and crash on a cycle of about 10 years (85%).
- Food: leafy plants in summer; twigs, buds and bark of shrubs and conifers in winter, clipped up to 45 cm above the snow (85%).
- Predators: lynx, fox, coyote, mink, great horned owl, goshawk (85%).

## Where and when near Heffley Creek

- Region 3 row, page 34: "SNOWSHOE HARE | 3-12 to 3-20, 3-26 to 3-44 | Aug 1 - Apr 30 | 10 (daily)" (98%)
- Young lodgepole pine, willow and alder thickets, cutblocks 10 to 20 years old, and swampy spruce edges (Tip).
- Dawn and dusk, and overcast days; in deep winter they sit in "forms" (packed beds) under low branches and move on the runways at night (75%).
- The season covers deer season; carry a small game licence mindset and a .22 in October (Tip).

## Other small game near home

- **Columbian ground squirrel:** Region 3 "No Closed Season, NBL", **private land only** with the landowner's permission (98%). Common on ranch pasture in spring and summer (Tip).
- **Raccoon:** Region 3 no closed season, no bag limit (98%). Raccoons carry a roundworm that can infect people; gloves and care with the gut (60%).
- **Skunk:** Schedule B; no Region 3 season row, so private land property protection only in Region 3 (90%).
- **Cottontails and rabbits:** eastern cottontail and European rabbit are Schedule C and live in introduced pockets of southwest BC, not in Region 3 (60%, VERIFY where). **Nuttall's cottontail** and **white tailed jackrabbit** in the Okanagan are protected: no season (98%).
- **Grey squirrels:** Schedule C; introduced in the Lower Mainland and on south Vancouver Island (70%). Native red squirrels are off limits to hunters (95%).

## The .22 rimfire: use it right

- Rimfire is legal for all small game and for grouse, ptarmigan and turkey among the birds (98%). Air guns and shotguns are legal too (98%).
- Hours: 1 hour before sunrise to 1 hour after sunset (98%).
- Head or neck shots at 25 to 50 m (27 to 55 yd) save all the meat (Tip). Wait for a sitting hare; a shotgun with 6 shot is better for running ones (Tip).
- A .22 bullet skips off frozen ground and water and travels over a kilometre; find a dirt [[Backstop]] every shot (Tip).
- Subsonic 40 grain hollow points are quiet and plenty for hares (Tip).
- No shooting within no shooting areas, from a vehicle, or across a road allowance (98%).
- Snaring hares is allowed in the open season with a hunting licence and the trapper education course; the snare must be unbraided wire no heavier than 20 gauge (98%).

"A person may snare snowshoe hare during the open season if they have a hunting licence and completed the trapper education course. The snare must be non-braided and no heavier than 20 gauge." (Synopsis page 13) (98%)

## Tactics: options

### Option A: still hunt the runways
- Walk slowly through young pine or willow, stop often, look for the black eye and ear tips of a sitting hare.
- Pros: works alone, any snow (Tip).
- Cons: hares freeze and you walk past them.

### Option B: two hunters and a push
- One walks the thicket, one stands on a runway crossing with a shotgun.
- Pros: hares circle back to their home range (Tip).
- Cons: zones of fire must be agreed before the first step.

### Option C: tracking after fresh snow
- Follow a fresh trail to the form, approach from downwind, shoot the sitting hare.
- Pros: teaches tracking for every other animal (Tip).
- Cons: slow.

> [!lean]
> Option A with the .22 on a snowy morning in December. Ten hares a day is a limit you will never need.

## Cleaning: tularemia caution

- Tularemia is caused by the bacterium Francisella tularensis; in BC it is reported occasionally, mostly in beavers, muskrats, voles, hares and rabbits (95%).
- BC has about 0 to 2 human cases a year (search preview) (75%).
- People catch it by handling live or dead animals, by tick bites, by eating undercooked meat, or from contaminated water (95%).
- Human signs: a persistent ulcer where the skin was cut, fever, chills, feeling ill, swollen lymph nodes; antibiotics work when it is caught (95%).
- The BC fact sheet rule: do not skin animals found dead (95%).
- Wear gloves and a mask while cleaning hares and rodents (85%). Wash hands and knives; cover cuts (Tip).
- Thoroughly cook wild game before eating (85%). A hare with a swollen, spotted liver or spleen goes in the bin, not the pot (hunter lore) (60%).
- A sick looking, slow hare is a hare you do not touch (95%).
- Check yourself for ticks after hunting brush in spring and fall (85%).

## Cooking

- Hare is lean, dark and strong; soak in salt water overnight, then braise or stew for 2 hours (Tip).
- Young hares fry; old hares stew (Tip).
- Cook until no pink remains at the bone (85%).
- Take the meat home: wasting the edible parts of game is unlawful (90%).

## Beginner mistakes

> [!mistake]
> - Shooting a .22 at a hare on a skyline or on ice with no backstop.
> - Skinning bare handed and nicking a finger.
> - Shooting a "rabbit" in the south Okanagan that was a protected Nuttall's cottontail.

## Grandpa's rules

> [!rule]
> A hare is a deer with the volume turned down. Hunt it the same way: slow, into the wind, eyes before feet.

> [!rule]
> Gloves on, every animal, every time.

> [!field]
> This week: walk a young pine cutblock after fresh snow, count the runways you cross in 200 m, and find one form.
