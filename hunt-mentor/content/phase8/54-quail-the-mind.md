> [!why]
> A covey thinks as a group: watch, call, run, burst, scatter, regroup. Learn the script and you know their next move before they make it.

```diagram mql-call-chart
```

- Quail talk all day: assembly, contact, alarm, advertising and fighting calls (70%).
- The assembly call, "chi CA go", is the one to learn first (70%).

```more the calls in full
- Assembly call: three syllables, loudest on the middle one ("cu CA cow" or "chi CA go"). Given when a bird is separated from the group or its mate, and before and during covey moves. Often repeated 10 or more times (70%, Edgewood notes from Calkins et al. 1999).
- Audubon writes it "ka kah ko" or "Chi ca go", the second note highest (75%). The BC atlas writes "put way doo" (85%).
- Contact calls: soft notes that keep a feeding covey together (70%). Washington Fish and Wildlife: listen for soft clucking while they feed (75%).
- Alarm: a quiet "pit pit" warning (60%, Okanagan naturalist in iNFOnews). The sentinel gives contact and aerial alarm calls (70%).
- Advertising call: a single loud "cow" from a high perch, by unpaired males early in the breeding season (70%).
- Fighting call: "squill", between males sorting out rank in spring (70%).
```

## Alarm and flight distance

```anim mql-run-then-fly A covey runs ahead inside the cover, then bursts at the end.
```

- First choice: run. Fly as a last resort (70%).
- Cover runs out, or you get too close: they burst (75%).

```more alarm and flight in full
- "Remain on ground and run very rapidly, flying as last resort." (70%, Birder's Handbook)
- If startled they explode into short, fast flight; given a choice, they escape on foot (70%).
- How close before they flush: no published number for BC birds. It depends on cover, pressure and the sentinel (Tip, VERIFY).
- Pressured birds near trees may fly up and perch in the branches (75%, Washington Fish and Wildlife).
- Hunter meaning: push them toward the end of the cover, not into a big thicket. Cover too big and they just run or stay in it (75%, Washington Fish and Wildlife).
```

## Scatter and regroup

```anim sb-covey-regroup The covey scatters, then calls and walks back together.
```

- A flushed covey scatters. Singles land a few dozen yards away and sit tight (75%).
- Then they call to regroup with the assembly call (70%).

```more scatter and regroup in full
- "When they scatter, quail may fly long distances, never to be seen again (at least not that day), or they may settle to the ground only a few dozen yards away" (75%, Washington Fish and Wildlife).
- "Those singles and doubles will sometimes hold very tight once they hit the ground" (75%).
- "When a covey is scattered, birds will often emit a sort of crowing whistle to locate each other, and a chorus of such calls may erupt around dusk, as birds begin to roost for the night." (75%)
- An Okanagan naturalist: about 10 minutes after a flush the covey has collected itself and is pecking again (Tip, one observer, iNFOnews).
- Assembly calls also start the covey's move off the night roost in the morning (70%, Edgewood notes).
```

## The regroup clock

```diagram mql-regroup-clock
```

- Flush, scatter, sit tight, call, regroup (70 to 75%).
- Late in the day, give them time to gather before dark (Tip).

```more why time matters
- Singles hold tight right after the flush: your best shooting chance on singles (75%).
- As calls start, birds move toward each other. Waiting too long means they are a covey again, and wary (Tip).
- Tall Timbers' Bill Palmer (bobwhite research): "The evidence suggests it's OK to hunt singles... They are so good at getting back together." (60%, bobwhite, Garden and Gun 2022)
- Same source, a manners rule: stop a bit before dark and give the birds time to gather (Tip).
```

## They learn pressure

```diagram mql-pressure
```

- Each flush teaches them. The second flush is farther out (75%).
- Hard hunted birds hold less and run more (60%).

```more pressure in full
- Gray partridge: "after they've been flushed once, they're likely to flush farther in front of you or your dog on the second approach, still farther out on the third." (75%, Washington Fish and Wildlife)
- Quail that have not been hunted hard may hold in small patches of cover instead of breaking into the open (75%).
- "Heavy hunting pressure can change the birds' habits, even moving them off an area." (60%, Missouri Conservationist 1996, bobwhite)
- Under raptor pressure, quail feed closer to cover and may skip the afternoon feed (70%, Edgewood notes).
- Hunter meaning: rest a covey. Hunt it once, then leave it a week (Tip).
```

## Social life: covey, rank and pairs

```diagram mql-covey-size
```

- Fall coveys: often under 30, sometimes 100 or more in BC (85%).
- Males hold a rank order. Only ranked males get mates (70%).

```more social life in full
- BC: coveys "occasionally include 100 or more birds, but more frequently less than 30" (85%, BC Breeding Bird Atlas).
- California: coveys average 20 to 25 (70%, Edgewood notes); up to 200, rarely over 300, in fall and winter (70%, Birder's Handbook).
- Washington: a dozen to several dozen birds (75%). Over 1,000 birds together has been reported in BC in fall and winter (Tip, search preview of Sierra Club BC, VERIFY).
- Families merge: broods join into "communal broods" with several adults (60%, Wikipedia summary).
- Males have dominance hierarchies that shape mate choice, relations between coveys and brood moves. Only birds in the hierarchy get mates. Dominant birds call more (70%, Edgewood notes).
- Pairs form from birds of the same covey. If both survive, they tend to pair again the next year (70%).
- In good years a female may lay a second clutch, leaving the first brood with a male (60%).
```

## Old birds and young birds

```diagram mql-survival
```

- Older birds pair earlier. Adult hens pick adult cocks (70%).
- Most fall birds are young: they have never seen a hunter (Tip).

```more old and young in full
- "Older birds generally mate earlier than younger birds, and adult females generally mate with adult males rather than yearling males." (70%, Edgewood notes)
- Few quail live past one year (65%). An old bird in your bag is uncommon (Tip).
- Tip: early October coveys full of young often hold better than the same coveys in late November after a few flushes.
```

## Their predictable mistakes

| They always... | So you... (Tip) |
|---|---|
| Call at dawn and dusk | Listen first, hunt next morning |
| Run to the end of cover | Be ready where it ends |
| Hold tight as singles | Hunt singles right after the flush |
| Dust and pick grit on lanes | Check lane edges midday and early |
| Come back to the same cover | Mark it in the Journal and on the map |

- Habits are strong; the covey is predictable (Tip).

```more where the table comes from
- Dawn and dusk calling: Edgewood notes and Washington Fish and Wildlife (70 to 75%).
- Running ahead in cover: Birder's Handbook (70%).
- Singles hold tight: Washington Fish and Wildlife (75%).
- Grit and dusting on road edges: Washington Fish and Wildlife (75%).
- Same small range: California winter home range of four coveys averaged 10.5 ha, from 6.9 to 18 ha (80%, California wildlife habitat records).
- "Quail often return to the same areas" (Tip, hunting articles).
```

## Memory hooks

```diagram mql-regroup-clock
```

- **Rhyme:** "Flush and scatter, sit and wait; then chi CA go calls up the mate." (Tip)
- **Number trick:** "10, 30, 10": regroup in about 10 min (Tip), coveys under 30 (85%), winter range about 10 ha (80%).

```more more hooks
- **Script:** watch, call, run, burst, scatter, regroup. Six words, the whole mind (70%).
- **Old hand's rule (opinion):** "Three birds from a covey, then let it go." A traditional manners rule quoted by Tall Timbers' Bill Palmer (Tip, 60%).
- **Old hand's rule (opinion):** "Listen at dusk, hunt at dawn." Widely repeated in quail hunting guides (Tip, Washington Fish and Wildlife describes the dusk chorus, 75%).
```
