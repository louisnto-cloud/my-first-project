> [!why]
> Grouse are creatures of habit with a few predictable tricks. Read the bird and you know when it will go, where it will land, and why the old ones are hard.

```diagram mgr-alarm-postures
```

- A tall neck and a raised crest: the bird is about to go (Tip).
- Clucks and a fanned tail often come first (Tip).

```more alarm signals
- Alert posture: neck stretched up, crest raised, head turning; the tail may fan (hunter lore, 60%).
- Alarm sounds: grouse cluck or "putt" before flushing, say many hunters (hunter lore, 55%: Tip). Exact calls not confirmed (VERIFY).
- A hen with chicks hisses, fans her tail and ruff, and may run around you or fake a broken wing to lead you off (70%, Macaulay Library recording notes and field accounts).
- A sooty or blue grouse hen with small chicks clucks, fluffs, droops her wings and may fly at an intruder (65%).
- In September a bird that walks off fast with a raised crest is not staying (Tip).
```

## Tame or wild

```diagram mgr-tame-scale
```

- Spruce grouse often sit; ruffed grouse go (75 to 80%).
- Every grouse gets warier after it is shot at (Tip).

```more what makes the difference
- Spruce grouse rely on stillness and camouflage and often let people approach closely: the "fool hen" (80%, Idaho Fish and Game).
- Dusky and sooty grouse are often approachable, especially young birds early in the season (Tip, 60%).
- Sharp tailed grouse: singles and small groups early in the season are easier; late season flocks flush far and seldom give a second chance (60%, hunting guides).
- Ruffed grouse: the wary one in most hunters' books (Tip).
- Habitat counts too: open cover means longer flushes; studies on a European grouse found less visual cover meant longer flush distances (70%).
```

## Hunting pressure and memory

```diagram mgr-road-pressure
```

- Birds near open roads get shot more (75%).
- Birds farther from roads live longer (75%).

```more the evidence
- Pennsylvania study: harvest rate was 48% near open roads versus 19% farther away (75%).
- Male survival was 36% beyond 201 m from roads versus 23% within 201 m (75%).
- A North Carolina study saw grouse change their movements to avoid areas after flushing and shooting (70%).
- Hunting pressure did not lower populations in that study's small woodlots from fall to fall (70%).
- Hinterland Who's Who: most hunted birds are young birds near roads and clearings (85%).
- Old hand's rule (tradition): "The easy birds live by the road. The good birds live past the first ridge."
```

## Families and loners

```diagram mgr-family-year
```

- Ruffed: hen raises the brood alone; males live alone (85%).
- At about 12 weeks the family breaks up and young birds move out (75%).

```more social life, species by species
- Ruffed grouse males hold a territory for life and drum to claim it (85%, Hinterland Who's Who). Hens range wider and pass through several males' areas (85%).
- Families break up when the young are about 12 weeks old (75%). Young hens moved about 4.8 km, young males about 2.1 km, over 17 to 21 days in Wisconsin (80%, Small and Rusch 1989).
- This "fall shuffle" makes young birds restless. Some fly into windows, fences and trees (75%, Pennsylvania Game Commission).
- Dusky grouse: summer groups of about 6 to 7 (a hen and chicks); winter groups of 15 to 20 seen (65%, Animal Diversity Web).
- Sharp tailed grouse: males gather on leks to display; in late fall birds bunch into big flocks (60 to 85%).
- Ptarmigan: in autumn young and adults form small flocks, which can merge (85%, Hinterland Who's Who). Male willow ptarmigan help raise the young, unlike other grouse (85%).
```

## Old birds and young birds

```diagram mgr-adult-share
```

- Early season: mostly naive young birds in the bag (75%).
- Late season: more adults, who know every escape (75%).

```more the old male
- Pennsylvania: adults were 34% of the harvest in October, 51% in November, 57% in December (75%).
- Adult males keep the same territory for life and know every escape route (85% territory; escape routes are hunter lore, Tip).
- An old bird often flushes wild, at the edge of range, with a tree between you (hunter lore, 60%).
- Young birds sit longer, fly into the open and land in plain sight (Tip).
- Old hand's rule (tradition): "September teaches the birds. November tests the hunter."
```

## Predictable mistakes grouse make

```anim mgr-follow-up Mark the tree. Go now.
```

- They glide a short way and land in a tree (75%).
- They return to the same food and grit spots (Tip).

```more mistakes you can use
- Short flight: most ruffed grouse fly under 90 m (100 yd) and land in trees (60 to 75%). Mark the spot and walk straight there.
- Walking away under an evergreen, then flushing from the far side: circle the tree (Tip, Outdoor Canada).
- Same spots: grit on the same road edge, dust in the same bowl, drum from the same log again and again (Tip; Hinterland Who's Who notes males hold territories for life, 85%).
- Young birds in September and October sit on road edges eating clover and grit (75%).
- Spruce grouse often stay put after the first shot (Tip). Be sure of your species and your bag count before the next.
```

## Memory hooks

```diagram mgr-tame-scale
```

- Rhyme: "Neck goes tall, bird goes all."
- Number trick: "12 weeks, then they seek": families split at about 12 weeks.
- Old hand's rule (tradition): "Mark the tree, go now."

```more more hooks
- "48 by the road, 19 off it": harvest rates near and away from roads.
- "Young birds in October, old birds in December."
- "Fool hen sits, ruffed hen splits."
- Rhymes are made for this course; the "mark and follow" habit is common advice in grouse hunting writing.
```
