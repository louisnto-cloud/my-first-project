## Bull, cow, calf

```diagram mc-moose-id
```

- Only bulls grow antlers (90%).
- Both sexes have a bell under the throat (90%).

```gallery
g-moose-bull-front | Bull: antlers spread like two open hands.
g-moose-cow-head | Cow: no antlers, long face, bell.
g-moose-cow-calf | Cow and calf: the calf is much smaller.
g-moose-calf-winter | Calf in its first winter: stubby face.
```

```more ID by sex and age, the full list
- **All moose** (90%, BC moose account 2000): longer legs than any other BC hoofed animal, a shoulder hump, dark brown to blackish coat, no rump patch, a bell (dewlap) of skin and hair under the throat, a large overhanging upper lip.
- **Bull:** antlers from late April or May to winter. Velvet in summer, hard from early September (90%). See chapter 5.
- **Cow:** no antlers. The synopsis definition: "a female moose recognizable by having no antlers and being 1.8 m at shoulder height weighing 340-420 kg" (99%, page 3, [definitions](#/s/rb-definitions)).
- **Calf** (under 12 months): "usually less than 1.5 m at shoulder height weighing 135-180 kg" (99%, page 3). Red brown at birth, adult like coat by late summer (85%, Alaska Department of Fish and Game).
- The synopsis warns that not every moose walking with a larger moose is a calf: "Be sure of your target" (99%, [definitions](#/s/rb-definitions)).
- A [[Bell]] does not prove a bull: both sexes carry one; bulls' are usually bigger (75%, Canadian Encyclopedia).
```

## Young bulls: the legal class

```diagram mc-moose-spikefork-odds
```

- Yearling bulls: 2 or 3 points a side, no real palm (90%).
- Fewer than half are spike fork (80%). Count every one.

```gallery
g-moose-young-bull | Young bull: spike and fork. Count each antler.
```

```more antlers, age and the spike fork rule
> [!warn]
> Study aid only. The official regulations are the law.

- BC account (90%): "Yearling bulls grow small antlers with two or three points on each side but with no significant palm." Older bulls usually grow palms, but antlers "are not a reliable means of determining the Moose's age."
- Child and others 2010, *Alces* 46, central BC, 1,886 bulls: the spike fork rule put about 46% of yearlings and about 16% of all bulls at risk (80%, abstract via search).
- Aitken and others 2021, *Alces* 57, province wide: about 43% of yearlings and 10% of 2 year olds are spike fork (75%, search preview of the paper).
- So more than half of all yearling bulls are **not** legal in a spike fork season (80%, my reading of the two studies).
- The law: a [[Spike fork bull]] has "no more than two tines (points) on one antler. (Includes tines on main antler and brow palms.) Does not include a calf." (99%, page 3, [definitions](#/s/rb-definitions)).
- Chapter 10 has the full rules recap. The Rule Book has the drawing: [Definitions](#/s/rb-definitions).
```

## Moose or elk?

```diagram mc-moose-lookalike
```

- Moose: no rump patch, bell, hump (90%).
- Not 100% sure what it is? No shot (Tip).

```more look alikes and the legal point
> [!warn]
> Study aid only. The official regulations are the law.

| Look alike | Tell it by | Legal point |
|---|---|---|
| Elk | Pale yellow rump patch, lighter body and darker neck, smaller, no bell (85%, [Elk](#/s/elk)) | No elk general open season in the Region 3 pages (95%, [the rut](#/s/the-rut)) |
| Cow moose | No antlers (99%) | Closed without an [[LEH]] (Limited Entry Hunting) draw in Regions 3 and 8 (99%) |
| Calf moose | Much smaller, stubby face (99%) | Same: closed without LEH (99%) |
| Horse, cow (cattle) | No hump, no bell, often near fences and ranches (Tip) | Never shoot a shape (Tip) |
| White moose | Pale coat; seen in the Omineca (99%, page 57) | A request not to shoot one, not a closure (95%, [Region 7A](#/s/rb-region-7a)) |

- The species quick lessons: [Moose](#/s/moose), [Elk](#/s/elk).
```

## Size and weight

```diagram mc-moose-size
```

- Bull: nearly 2 m at the shoulder, 450 to 500 kg (90%).
- Cow: 1.8 m, 340 to 420 kg (99%).

```more size details and what it means for you
- BC account (90%): autumn adult cows average about 340 to 420 kg; adult bulls 450 to 500 kg; the heaviest recorded is 595 kg, "but some bulls are undoubtedly heavier".
- Bulls stand "nearly 2 m tall at the shoulder" and carry the biggest antlers in the deer family (90%).
- Southeast BC moose have the smallest bodies and antlers; the biggest live in northern BC (90%).
- Alaska figures for the biggest race: bulls 542 to 725 kg, cows 364 to 591 kg (85%, Alaska Department of Fish and Game). Not your Kamloops moose.
- Newborn calves: 11 to 16 kg (90%). By early winter 135 to 180 kg (90%).
- What it means for you: even a young bull's four quarters weigh about 160 kg (75%, chapter 10). Two people, a plan, and a full day (Tip).
```

## Three races in BC

```diagram mc-moose-subspecies
```

- One species, Alces alces, three races in BC (90%).
- Kamloops and the Okanagan: Northwestern moose (80%).

```more the three races
- BC account (90%): three subspecies, all similar in looks:
  - **Northwestern moose** (Alces alces andersoni): most of the province.
  - **Alaskan moose** (A. a. gigas): the far northwest. The biggest moose.
  - **Shiras moose** (A. a. shirasi): the far southeast corner. The smallest bodies and antlers.
- The account map shows Shiras moose east of a line in the southeast Rockies and Alaskan moose in the northwest corner; Kamloops and the Okanagan sit in Northwestern moose range (80%, my reading of the map).
- Canada has a fourth race in the east, A. a. americana (75%, Canadian Encyclopedia).
- Moose arrived from Asia over the Bering land bridge in the last ice age (90%, BC account). "In Europe they are called elk" (85%, Alaska Department of Fish and Game).
```

## Where moose live in BC

```diagram sa-moose-where
```

- Most of the Interior; plentiful in the north (90%).
- Scarce in dry valleys like the Thompson (90%).

```more distribution in detail
- BC account (90%): moose are "most abundant in the central and sub boreal interior, the northern boreal mountains, and the boreal plains of northeastern British Columbia", and common in mountain valleys "except for a few dry southern valleys like the Thompson and Okanagan."
- They are usually absent from the coast, but reach tidewater at the heads of some inlets from Bute Inlet north (90%).
- Over 70% of BC moose lived in northern BC in 2000 (90%).
- Winter density in BC: 0.3 to 1.5 moose per km² (90%). The Bonaparte study area (MUs 3-29 and 3-30, next to Heffley Creek) counted 0.30 per km² in 2012 and 0.25 in 2017 (90%, BC moose research report 2022).
- What it means near Kamloops: look on the forested plateaus with lakes, wetlands, burns and cutblocks, not on the dry grass benches (85%, my reading). Chapter 7 has the detail.
```

## How many, and the trend

```diagram mc-moose-numbers
```

- BC: 115,000 to 192,000 moose (2022, 90%).
- Region 3: 7,000 to 9,000, stable (90%).

```more numbers, trend and why
- BC 2022 provincial ungulate estimates (90%, read directly): Thompson 7,000 to 9,000, stable; Okanagan 4,000 to 5,000, stable to increasing; Lower Mainland 70 to 100, stable; province 115,000 to 192,000.
- Moose are counted from aircraft by MU (Management Unit), aiming for each key MU once every 5 years (90%, same document).
- Past numbers: about 170,000 in 2000 (90%, BC account); a high of 190,000 in 2011 and a low of 148,000 in 2017 (85%, BC winter tick report 2021 citing Kuzyk).
- Moose fell sharply in parts of the central Interior in the early 2000s, at the same time as the mountain pine beetle outbreak, salvage logging and new roads (90%, BC moose research report 2022).
- The study's idea (the "landscape change hypothesis"): open cutblocks and roads made moose easier for hunters and predators to find (90%). Cow survival turned out normal (over 85% most years); calf survival and recruitment matter more (85%).
- BC harvest in 2000: 8,000 to 14,000 moose a year, "more meat than all other ungulates combined" (90%, BC account).
```

## Memory hooks

```diagram mc-moose-id
```

- **Rhyme:** "Hump and bell, no white behind: that's the moose you're bound to find."
- **Number trick:** 2, 18, 340: about 2 m bull; 1.8 m cow; cow 340 to 420 kg (90 to 99%).
- **Old hand's rule** (tradition): "Antlers first, body second, trigger last."

```more more hooks
- **Rhyme:** "Spike or fork on one side? Count it twice before you ride." (Ride the trigger: shoot.)
- **Number trick:** 3 races: Alaskan big in the northwest, Shiras small in the southeast, Northwestern everywhere between (90%).
- **Number trick:** 8 and 77: about 8% of MU 3-27 moose hunters succeed; about 77 hunter days per moose (90%, computed).
- **Number trick:** 43 to 46: the share of yearling bulls that are spike fork (75 to 80%). More than half are not.
- **Old hand's rule** (widely taught hunter education wisdom, opinion): "If you are not sure, you are not shooting."
- **Old hand's rule** (opinion): "No antlers, no bang" in a spike fork season.

> [!mistake]
> - Calling a 3 point yearling "small enough".
> - Taking a big calf for a young bull: calves never count as a spike fork bull.
> - Expecting moose on the dry grass benches near Kamloops.

> [!field]
> Next drive north of Kamloops at dusk: for every moose you see, say "bull, cow or calf", then count the points on one antler.
```
