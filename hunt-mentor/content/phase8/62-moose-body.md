## Frame and hump

```diagram vitals-moose
```

```photo bg-moose-bell
```

- Long legs carry a body over logs, muskeg and deep snow (90%).
- The hump fools your eye: aim low (Tip).

```more the frame in detail
- BC account (90%): moose have "long legs to help them travel through fallen timber, muskeg, and deep snow".
- The shoulder hump: bones in the hump connect with muscle and tendons that hold up the heavy head and, in bulls, the antlers (75%, Canadian Encyclopedia). It sits above the spine, not over the lungs.
- The chest looks enormous from the side because of the hump and long hair. The lungs fill only the lower part behind the front leg (75%, standard hunter education; see the [vitals drawing](#/s/shot-placement)).
- Heavy shoulder bone and big muscle in front: a quartering to shot needs a tough bullet and experience. Beginner: wait for broadside (Tip, [Moose](#/s/moose)).
- Hooves: long split toes and dewclaws that spread in mud and snow (75%, chapter 8).
```

## Lungs, heart and breathing

```anim mc-moose-breath The diaphragm pulls back: air rushes in.
```

- Two big lungs, ahead of the diaphragm (85%).
- Both lungs hit: the moose runs out of air fast (85%).
- The heart sits low, between the front legs (85%).

```more how breathing works
- Like every mammal, a moose breathes with a sheet of muscle, the diaphragm, that pulls back to fill the lungs (85%, basic anatomy).
- The diaphragm runs from the bottom of the ribs up and back to the spine. Everything ahead of it (lungs, heart) is the vital zone; everything behind it (liver, rumen, gut) is a slow death (85%, [shot placement](#/s/shot-placement)).
- A double lung hit collapses both lungs; blood loss and no air drop the animal, often within about 100 m for deer (85%, app shot placement session). Moose often walk or trot a short way and stand (Tip, [Moose](#/s/moose)).
- In field dressing you cut the diaphragm to reach the lungs and heart (Tip, [field dressing](#/s/field-dressing)).
```

```more heart in detail
- Heart: low in the chest, just behind and above the point of the front elbow (85%, [shot placement](#/s/shot-placement)).
- A heart shot often gives a hard run, then a crash; lungs a shorter run; both are fatal fast (70%, [blood trailing](#/s/blood-trailing)).
- The big vessels (aorta and its branches) run along the top of the chest under the spine. A shot just under the spine can still be fatal, but it is not the aim point (Tip).
- Shooting low into the heart risks a leg hit if you are too low. Aim one third up the chest (85%, chapter 10).
```

## The four part stomach

```diagram mc-moose-rumen
```

- A browser: twigs and leaves, not grass (90%).
- Up to 20 kg of food a day in winter (90%).

```video
commons-moose-bull | A bull browsing: twigs and leaves, not grass.
```

```more digestion in detail
- Moose are ruminants: a four part stomach (rumen, reticulum, omasum, abomasum). Microbes in the rumen break down woody food; the moose brings cud back up to chew again (85%).
- BC account (90%): moose "are better adapted to a diet of coarse woody browse than are other hoofed animals within their range". They "eat up to 20 kg of food every day in winter".
- *Alces* journal review: moose are seasonally adaptable "concentrate" (foliage) selectors; the omasum is very small and all stomach openings are wide, which speeds food passage (70%, abstract via search).
- Twig size matters: most nutrients sit in the twig surface, so thicker twigs give less value (70%, *Alces* abstract).
- Captive moose fed about 10 hours a day in about 5 bouts; rumination ran from 12.6 hours a day in midwinter to 2.8 hours in May (70%, Renecker and Hudson 1989 via search).
- What it means for you: the rumen fills the belly behind the diaphragm. A shot there spills it, taints meat and leads to a long track (85%). Aim forward.
```

## Built for cold

```diagram mc-moose-heat
```

- Summer: breathing speeds up from about 14°C (70%).
- Warm afternoons: moose bed in shade or water (75%).

```more heat in detail
- Renecker and Hudson 1986, two captive moose in Alberta: in summer breathing rate rose from about 14°C and open mouth panting began about 20°C; in late winter signs of heat stress started near minus 5°C (70%, as cited in later papers).
- Later work found higher summer thresholds (about 17°C without wind, 24°C with wind) and warns there is no single number for wild moose (70%, McCann and others 2013 via search).
- Winter coat: long guard hairs over fine wool let moose survive "the coldest climates on earth" (90%, BC account).
- What it means for you: a warm October afternoon in the Interior can top 14°C. Expect moose bedded in shade, timber or wet ground in the heat of the day (70%; Tip).
- It also means the carcass holds heat. Chapter 10: skin and quarter fast.
```

## Fat through the year

```diagram mc-moose-fat-year
```

- Bulls burn their fat in the rut (85%).
- Cows are thinnest after winter, April to June (85%).

```more fat in detail
- Alaska Department of Fish and Game (85%): "By late October, adult males have exhausted their summer accumulation of fat and their desire for female company. Once again they begin feeding."
- Central BC carcass study: adult bulls were heavier before the rut than after it; calves and cows did not change between October and late November seasons (75%, Aitken and others 2012 via search).
- BC collared cows: most deaths in poor body condition or acute malnutrition came April to June (90%, BC moose research report 2022). Body fat entering winter was about 8 to 10% (90%).
- Winter: moose eat less and lose weight; deep crusted snow can starve hundreds (85%, Alaska Department of Fish and Game).
- What it means for you: a November spike fork bull is still in fair shape, but lean. Add fat when you grind (Tip).
```

## Legs and snow

```diagram mc-moose-snow
```

```photo bg-moose-snow
```

- Easy to 40 cm of snow; slight trouble at 40 to 70 cm (90%).
- Moose often winter where snow is 50 cm or more (90%).

```more snow in detail
- BC account (90%): field studies showed "little or no difficulty moving around in snow up to 40 cm deep and only slight difficulty in snow depths of 40 to 70 cm deep". Snow density also matters.
- "Moose commonly winter in areas with 50 cm or more of snow cover, which usually excludes other ungulates except Caribou" (90%).
- Deer have trouble past about 30 cm (85%, [Mule deer Masterclass 2](#/s/mc-mule-deer-02)).
- What it means for you: snow pushes deer down long before moose. Do not expect moose to follow the deer to low ground in early November (85%, chapter 5).
```

## Winter tick

```diagram mc-moose-tick-cycle
```

- One tick, one moose, one winter (85%).
- Tens of thousands on a moose; over 100,000 in extreme cases (85%).

```gallery
hab-moose-willows | Winter browse: a tick hit moose eats less.
```

```more winter tick in detail
- Winter tick (Dermacentor albipictus), also called "moose tick", finishes its whole life on one host (85%, BC winter tick report 2021).
- Timing (85%): larvae climb on during the rut, mid September to mid October; first blood meal in October; dormant until January; second meal; adult females engorge March to April, then drop to lay thousands of eggs.
- Effects (85%): blood loss and anaemia, less growth in young moose, less fat; constant grooming breaks and rubs off the winter coat, which costs heat and feeding time. With a hard winter it can kill, mostly calves and weak moose.
- Elk, deer, caribou and bison are less affected thanks to grooming, immunity and coat (85%).
- Tick numbers rise with mild, short spring snow and dry falls; cold snow at drop off can kill females (85%).
- BC 2021 survey: 19% of moose seen showed some hair loss, down from 31% in 2020 (85%). Bonaparte cows carried about 4 ticks per transect count in 2021 to 2022 (90%, BC moose research report).
- "Winter ticks pose no health risk to humans" (85%, BC government news release 2018).
- Most BC moose do not die from parasites (90%, BC account). Other parasites named: liver flukes and tapeworm cysts (90%).
```

## The ghost moose

```diagram mc-moose-ghost
```

```photo bg-moose-ghost
```

- Grey patches on neck, shoulders and rump: tick hair loss (85%).
- Seen January to April; report it (85%).

```more reporting and what it means for you
- Hair loss shows most on the neck, shoulders, upper mane, withers and hind quarters (85%, BC winter tick report).
- The survey scores five classes, from no hair loss to more than 80% of winter hair gone (a "ghost" moose) (85%).
- Report sightings to the BC moose winter tick survey; the BC Moose Tracker app also logs moose you see (99%, [after the kill](#/s/rb-after-the-kill)).
- During your November hunt the ticks are small larvae and nymphs, so you will rarely see hair loss then (75%, my reading of the timing).
- Liver flukes or cysts in the organs: look and ask a conservation officer if unsure (Tip, chapter 10).
```

## Lifespan and age

```diagram mc-moose-antler-year
```

```photo bg-moose-velvet-early
```

- Wild moose rarely live past 16 years (85%).
- BC collared cows died at 10.4 years on average, up to 18 (90%).

```more age in detail
- Alaska Department of Fish and Game (85%): "Moose rarely live more than 16 years."
- BC collared cows, 2012 to 2022: average age at death 10.4 years, range 1 to 18 (90%, BC moose research report 2022).
- Antlers keep growing until about age 10 (90%, BC account); Alaska says the largest at about 10 to 12 (85%).
- Cows usually first breed at about 2 years (90%). Bulls can breed as yearlings, but older bulls do most of it (90%).
- Causes of death of BC collared cows: 58% predation, mostly wolves (bears 7%, cougars 3%), 17% health, 11% hunting (90%).
- Age on the hoof is hard; body clues and antler size are guides only (Tip). Chapter 4 has old bull behaviour.
```

## Memory hooks

```diagram mc-moose-body-cutaway
```

- **Rhyme:** "Hump on top is only meat; aim low where heart and lungs beat."
- **Number trick:** 40, 70: easy in 40 cm of snow, slight trouble to 70 (90%).
- **Old hand's rule** (tradition): "Shoot the moose's chest, not its hump."

```more more hooks
- **Rhyme:** "Ticks climb on when bulls call; grey ghost moose by spring's thaw."
- **Number trick:** 14 and 20: summer breathing speeds up at about 14°C, panting at 20°C (70%).
- **Number trick:** 20 kg: winter food a day (90%).
- **Number trick:** 16 and 18: rarely past 16 years; oldest BC collared cow 18 (85 to 90%).
- **Old hand's rule** (widely taught, opinion): "Behind the diaphragm is a long night."

> [!mistake]
> - Aiming at the middle of the dark body: that is the rumen.
> - Aiming at the centre of hump plus chest: too high, over the lungs.
> - Assuming moose stay in the open on a warm afternoon.

> [!field]
> Look at the [vitals drawing](#/s/shot-placement) and the moose photo in chapter 1. Point to the back of the front leg, then one third up. Do it 10 times until it is automatic.
```
