## Alarm signals

```steps 5 stages of alarm
sg-alarm-1 | Alert: head up, ears forward, stare.
sg-alarm-2 | Head bob: drops to feed, snaps up.
sg-alarm-3 | Foot stamp: wants you to move.
sg-alarm-4 | Snort: whole group alert.
sg-alarm-5 | Escape: the stot.
```

- Stage 1 or 2? Freeze and you may win (Tip).
- Stiff, jerky walk = it has noticed you (80%).

```more each signal and what to do
- **Stare:** head up, ears forward. Freeze until it looks away (Tip).
- **Head bob:** drops the head as if to feed, then snaps it up to catch you moving (hunter lore, 70%).
- **Foot stamp:** a front hoof stamp warns other deer and tries to make you move (70%).
- **Snort or blow:** a loud whoosh through the nose; all deer nearby go on alert (70%, mostly white tail sources).
- **Stiff, jerky walk:** nervous mule deer walk "in a stiff and jerky manner" (80%, US National Park Service).
- **Alarm scent:** the metatarsal gland on the hind leg releases alarm scent (90%, BC account).
- **Tail:** a mule deer does not flag like a white tail; a tail held out or flicked while it stares means tension (Tip).
- Stared at? Do not move a finger. Count to 100. Most deer go back to feeding if nothing else happens (Tip).
```

## The stot uphill and the look back

```anim mmd-stot-lookback Stot uphill, stop on the rise, look back.
```

- Mule deer flee uphill into rough ground (70%).
- Many stop on the rise and look back (65%).

```more the stot and the look back
- The **stot**: all four feet land and push off together. Each bound covers 2.7 to 5.2 m, up to about 40 km/h (80%, US National Park Service).
- Stotting suits steep, broken, brushy ground; it may also let the deer see over brush on its escape route (60%, Wild About Utah summary).
- In Alberta studies, mule deer confronted by coyotes moved up slopes; white tails ran down and away (70%, Lingle, search previews).
- The **look back**: after a few hundred metres a mule deer often stops on a rise to check on what spooked it (65%, Rinella; Outdoor Life; hunter consensus).
- Big, mature bucks are often the exception: they leave and keep going (65%, Rinella).
- What to do: when a deer stots off, do not chase. Get a rest, find the skyline it will cross, and wait. Only shoot a standing, broadside deer you have fully identified (Tip).
```

## Flight distance and pressure

```diagram mmd-flight-rings
```

- Hunted deer run from farther away (65%).
- Off trail walkers and dogs scare deer most (85%).

```more flight distance, pressure and what deer do about it
- A 2008 review of hoofed animals: hunted populations flee people at greater distances than unhunted ones; predictable disturbance gets a smaller response than surprise (65%, Stankowich 2008 via citing papers).
- People off trail cause more avoidance than people on trails; people with dogs cause even more (85%, Mule Deer Working Group fact sheet 17).
- "If deer are moving away from you, you are too close" (85%, fact sheet 17).
- Under hunting pressure, mule deer studied in Arizona did not leave their home ranges; they moved into better escape cover (65%). A Montana study found more use of timber in hunting season (65%).
- Roads and access raise buck harvest risk: Idaho names open road density as the biggest driver, plus less hiding cover, gentle terrain and hunting during the rut (65%, Idaho Fish and Game, search preview).
- Mule deer are more open country deer and more vulnerable to harvest than white tails (65%, Montana Fish, Wildlife and Parks policy).
- For you: walk 500 m to 1 km past roads; close the truck door with your hip; no voices (Tip).
```

## How it learns and remembers

```diagram mmd-learning-loop
```

- Same migration route every year (85%).
- Learned places pass from mother to fawn (85%).

```more memory, learning and habit
- GPS (Global Positioning System) collars show mule deer use the same migration route year after year (85%, Mule Deer Working Group fact sheet 12).
- Most SIMDeer (Southern Interior Mule Deer Project) deer "choose nearly the same path" each year (85%, BC Wildlife Federation 2020 update).
- Deer return to the same home range year after year (85%, research notes). Mature deer are highly faithful to their ranges; yearling males are not (60%, Montana study, search preview).
- Learned behaviour passes on: winter fed deer learn feeding sites and share them with each year's offspring (85%, fact sheet 2).
- A deer spooked from one spot returns to its range; spooking is not forever (85%, quick lesson). But it may use that spot only at night for a while (Tip).
- Habit you can use: the same trails, the same benches, the same crossing in the same wind (Tip).
```

## Social life

```diagram mmd-social
```

- Doe clans: related females. Bucks: unrelated (90%).
- Sparring is bonding, not fighting (90%).

```more groups, rank and the rut
- "The social system consists of clans of females that are related to each other by maternal descent and bucks that are not related" (90%, BC deer account).
- Mule deer mostly travel alone or in small groups, but "sometimes form larger groups" (90%). Groups are mostly 5 or fewer; winter 5 to 8, up to 15 to 30 (75%, research notes).
- Bucks spend summer in bachelor groups of 2 to 4; these break up in October (80%).
- Dominance display: a dominant buck circles a rival with deliberate steps, back arched, head low, tail flicking; the smaller one often bolts (90%).
- Bucks also show dominance by "violently thrashing the bushes with their antlers" (90%).
- Sparring between unequal bucks, especially yearlings and 2 year olds, is pushing and twisting, and "actually result[s] in social bonding" (90%).
- In the rut, mature bucks of equal size fight head to head (90%). Older dominant bucks do most of the breeding; does cluster around the largest bucks (90%).
- Does in groups have more eyes and ears (85%). When you glass one deer, look for the others (Tip).
```

## Mother and fawn

```diagram mmd-fawn-hide
```

- Fawns hide over 95% of the time; mother stays near (85%).
- Mule deer does fight coyotes to defend fawns (70%).

```more fawn life and the doe's defence
- Newborn fawns are "hiders": alone, licked clean, spotted, still (85%, Mule Deer Working Group fact sheet 26).
- Days 1 to 3: head and ears flat, motionless even if touched. Days 4 to 6: hide, then bolt. By a week: run from farther. At 2 to 3 weeks: follow the mother (85%).
- The doe nurses a few times a day and stays a few hundred metres away (85%). Deer milk has nearly 3 times the fat and protein of cow milk; fawns gain 5 to 10% of their birth weight a day (85%).
- Before giving birth, does drive off last year's young and find a secluded spot (90%, BC account). Twins are the rule; young does often have one (90%).
- Mule deer does vigorously defend fawns against coyotes; white tails rely more on flight (70%, Lingle, American Naturalist 2008, search preview).
- Mule deer and white tail mothers respond to recorded fawn distress calls, even of other species in the right pitch (70%, Lingle 2007 and 2014).
- Found a fawn? Leave it. The mother is near (85%, fact sheet 26).
- Most fawn deaths in the first 15 weeks in SIMDeer were bears; cougars led for adult does and older fawns (85%, BC Wildlife Federation 2021 update).
```

## Old buck, young buck

```diagram mmd-old-vs-young
```

- Young bucks wander and look back (60 to 65%).
- Old bucks hold tight and slip away (65%).

```more personality by age
- Yearling males are not faithful to a range; mature deer are (60%, Montana).
- Young bucks are easier to see: daylight, open slopes, curious, look back (Tip, hunter consensus).
- Big mature bucks often do not look back (65%, Rinella).
- After the rut, bucks are thin and "tend to hide and rest" (90%, BC account).
- By about 4 years antlers are 80 to 90% of peak size (85%, fact sheet 24). A big old buck may not carry the biggest rack.
- Older bucks drop antlers first in winter (90%, BC account).
- Buck survival is lower where roads are dense and cover thin (65%, Idaho).
- Old hand's view (opinion): old bucks bed where they see below and smell above, often alone, and move in the last minutes of light (Tip).
```

## Predictable mistakes

```diagram mmd-learning-loop
```

- The look back, curiosity, and the rut (60 to 75%).
- Same trails, same benches, same time (Tip).

```more the list an old hand uses
- **The look back** on a rise (65%).
- **Curiosity:** a deer that cannot place a sound may walk closer to see it (Tip).
- **Routine:** same trails, crossings and beds when undisturbed (85% for routes; Tip for daily beds).
- **First light in the open:** feeding on open slopes at dawn, then drifting to the bed (85%, chapter 6).
- **The rut:** bucks travel in daylight and focus on does (75%, the rut session). "Without a doubt, the greatest time to hunt mule deer is during the rut" (65%, Rinella).
- **Snow:** snow pushes them down onto winter range, where they gather (85%).
- **Group trust:** a relaxed doe group calms a buck; spook one and they all go (Tip).
- Use each one legally and fairly: no chasing with vehicles, no lights (99%, [legal methods](#/s/rb-legal-methods)).
```

## Memory hooks

```diagram mmd-old-vs-young
```

- **Rhyme:** "Up the hill, then stand and stare; old bucks don't, so be aware."
- **Number trick:** 95% hiding fawn, few hundred metres to mum (85%).
- **Old hand's rule** (tradition): "When a mulie bounces off, sit and count to 60."

```more more hooks
- **Rhyme:** "Cougar fear made the mule deer: high and open, eyes in the rear."
- **Number trick:** 2.7 to 5.2: metres per bound in a stot; 40 km/h top (80%).
- **Number trick:** 1 clan of does, 2 to 4 bachelor bucks, groups mostly 5 or fewer (75 to 90%).
- **Old hand's rule** (widely told, opinion): "Big bucks don't look back."
- **Old hand's rule** (opinion): "If they are moving away, you are too close." (Also fact sheet 17 advice, 85%.)

> [!mistake]
> - Chasing a deer that stotted over the hill.
> - Moving during the head bob.
> - Expecting an old buck to behave like a young one.

> [!field]
> Next time a deer stots away: stay put, rifle on a rest, watch the next rise for 2 minutes. Note if it looked back.
```
