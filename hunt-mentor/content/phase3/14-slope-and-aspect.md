> [!why]
> Steepness decides where animals feel safe. The way a slope faces decides how warm it is, what grows, and where snow lies.

- **Slope**: how steep the ground is.
- **Aspect**: which way the slope faces (95%).

```more what this lesson covers

- This lesson: degrees and percent, reading steepness on the map and the Hunt Map, why south faces are warm in October, what grows on each face, how animals use each face by season and time of day, and safety on steep ground.
```

## Slope: degrees and percent

```diagram rl-sa-slope-scale
```

- Percent = rise divided by run, times 100 (98%).
- 45 degrees is 100 percent (98%).

```more the conversion table
| Degrees | Percent | Feels like (Tip) |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | 9 | Gentle walk |
| 10 | 18 | Steady walk |
| 15 | 27 | Hard with a pack |
| 20 | 36 | Steep, short steps |
| 25 | 47 | Very steep, zig zag |
| 30 | 58 | Hands sometimes |
| 35 | 70 | Scramble |
| 40 | 84 | Hands and care |
| 45 | 100 | Climbing |

- Percent = tan(degrees) x 100 (98%, trigonometry). The two scales are not the same: 30 degrees is 58 percent, not 30.
- Forestry and wildlife reports usually give slope in percent. Avalanche and climbing guides use degrees (85%). Check which one a source means.
- The feel column is a beginner guide, not a measurement (Tip).
```

## Count the lines

```diagram rl-sa-count-lines
```

- On the Hunt Map each contour line is 20 m of height (99%, app).
- Lines per 100 m of ground x 20 = slope in percent (95%).

```more how to measure slope on the Hunt Map
- Use Measure (in Tools) or the scale bar at the top left to mark 100 m. Count the contour lines crossing it (Tip).
- Each line is 20 m of rise in metres mode, so 1 line is about 20 percent, 2 lines 40 percent, 5 lines 100 percent (95%, maths).
- On a 1:50,000 paper map with 20 m lines, count lines per cm instead: 5 lines per cm is 100 m over 500 m, 20 percent. See the table in [Maps 1](#/s/maps-terrain/0).
- Faster: turn on Slope steepness and read the colour (next screen).
```

## Slope on the Hunt Map

```diagram rl-sa-hm-keys
```

- Yellow, orange, red, purple: steeper and steeper (99%, app).
- No colour on a coloured face: a bench or flat (Tip).

```more both colour keys in words
- Slope steepness: under 15 degrees no colour, 15 to 25 yellow (steady climb), 25 to 30 orange (steep), 30 to 45 red (very steep, avalanche terrain in snow), over 45 purple (cliffy, scrambling) (99%, app key).
- Slope direction (aspect): north blue, northeast teal, east green, southeast lime, south yellow, southwest orange, west red, northwest purple. Ground flatter than 8 degrees gets no colour (99%, app key).
- Both are worked out on the phone from terrain samples about 24 m apart. Small cliffs, cut banks and rock bands do not show (95%, app notes).
- Both work offline in areas you saved with Offline Maps (99%, app).
```

## Aspect: which way it faces

```diagram rl-sa-aspect-compass
```

- Aspect is the way water would run off the slope (95%).
- A south slope faces south: it looks at the sun.

```more how to read aspect on any map
- Picture water poured on the slope: the direction it runs downhill is the aspect (95%).
- On a topo map, aspect is at right angles to the contour lines, toward the lower numbers (95%).
- On the Hunt Map, turn on Slope direction: the colour tells you the face at a glance (99%, app).
- On the ground, use your compass: face straight downhill and read the bearing (95%).
- Forestry guides group aspects: warm is about 135 to 270 degrees (southeast through west), cool about 315 to 90 degrees (northwest through east) (85%, BC silviculture practices for mule deer winter range, 2004).
```

## Why south faces are warm in October

```diagram rl-sa-sun-angle
```

- Mid October noon sun near Kamloops: about 30 degrees up (95%).
- A 30 degree north face gets almost no direct sun at noon (90%).

```more the sun maths
- Noon sun height = 90 minus your latitude plus the sun's declination (98%, astronomy).
- Heffley Creek is about 50.9 degrees north (99%, app base point). Mid October the declination is about minus 9 degrees, so the noon sun is about 30 degrees up (95%). On 21 December it is about 16 degrees (95%).
- A south slope tilted 30 degrees toward that sun meets it at about 60 degrees: about 87 percent of full strength. Flat ground gets about 50 percent. A north slope tilted 30 degrees away gets almost nothing (90%, simple noon geometry; real slopes and times vary).
- In October the sun rises south of east and sets south of west (90%), so a steep north face stays in shade all day.
- Result: south and west faces dry out, melt snow first and grow grass. North faces stay cool and moist and grow thick timber (80%).
```

## What grows where

| Face | Near Kamloops (typical) |
|---|---|
| South, southwest | Bunchgrass, sagebrush, open Douglas fir, ponderosa pine (75%) |
| West | Warm and dry, open timber (70%) |
| East | Mixed, first morning sun (Tip) |
| North, northeast | Thick Douglas fir and spruce, moss, late snow (75%) |

```gallery
hab-lac-du-bois-grassland | Open bunchgrass: warm, dry faces.
hab-douglas-fir-stand | Thick Douglas fir: cooler, shadier faces.
```

- Drive any valley and you see it: grass on one side, timber on the other (Tip).

```more why the plants split
- Warm aspects lose more water to sun, so trees are fewer and grass and shrubs take over (75%).
- North aspects "support denser stands of higher crown closures" (85%, BC wildlife habitat rating account, mule deer, 2004).
- In the Thompson, valley bottoms and warm faces carry bunchgrass, cool faces carry Douglas fir first (75%). See [Habitat tour: deer](#/s/habitat-deer/0).
- The same split shows on the Hunt Map Satellite base: pale grass on one face, dark timber on the facing slope (Tip).
```

## How animals use aspect through the year

| Animal | Warm south and west faces | Cool north faces |
|---|---|---|
| Mule deer | Winter range, spring green up (85%) | Hot day beds, deep snow refuge under thick timber (75%) |
| White tailed deer | Winter on low warm slopes and floodplains (80%) | Thick cover beds (Tip) |
| Bighorn sheep | Winter on steep south to southwest grass (85%) | Rarely (Tip) |
| Mountain goat | Warm aspect winter range (85%) | Shade in summer heat (70%) |
| Black bear | Spring green up (Tip, app) | Cool shade, berries later (Tip) |
| Chukar, quail | South slopes in snow (75%) | Rarely (Tip) |

- The colder it gets, the more animals crowd onto warm faces (80%).

```more sources for each row
- Mule deer: spring feeding "on moderate to steep, south and west facing slopes"; winter in Douglas fir "with south facing, warm aspect slopes where the snowpack is lower"; north aspects used "during severe weather conditions" because their thick timber catches snow (85%, BC wildlife habitat rating account, mule deer, 2004).
- Mule deer winter range definition: "10 to 45 % slope, having a south and/or west aspect, and below 1500 m" in shallow to moderate snow zones (85%, same account citing US Forest Service 1998).
- Mule deer summer beds: in Montana and Idaho study sites, deer chose cooler northeast and east aspects with overhead cover (60%, thesis via search preview).
- White tails: winter on "low, south facing warm slopes or floodplains" (80%, BC account, as logged in research/2026-10-03).
- Bighorn sheep: winter ranges "tend to be steep, south to southwest facing" bunchgrass (85%, Bighorn Sheep in BC, 2000).
- Mountain goat: "warm aspect" is a main factor in winter range choice (85%, BC Mountain Goat Management Plan, 2010).
- Chukar: may move onto south facing slopes in heavy snow (75%, USDA Fire Effects Information System).
- Black bear spring: Hunt Map Slope direction tip (Tip).
```

## Aspect through one day

```anim rl-sa-day Sun in the morning, shade at noon, feed at dusk.
```

- Cold morning: deer often bed where the first sun hits (Tip).
- Warm midday: they slide into shade (Tip).

```more daily aspect pattern
- On cold sunny days, deer often bed where the first sun hits, then move into shade as it warms up (Tip, app Sun and shade tool note).
- Glass east faces at sunrise and sunny faces early. Glass shady timber edges in the heat of the day (Tip).
- On warm October afternoons, west faces get the hottest sun: animals bed elsewhere until evening (Tip).
- The Hunt Map Sun and shade tool lights the map by the real sun for any time today, and shows sunrise and sunset at the map centre (99%, app).
```

## Slope and how animals feel safe

```diagram hf-slope-profile
```

- Steep, broken ground is security cover for mule deer (85%).
- Gentle ground is easy feeding and easy travel (Tip).

```more slope and animals in detail
- "Areas of steep broken terrain can also be used as security habitat" for mule deer (85%, BC habitat account citing Nyberg and Janz 1990).
- When coyotes come, mule deer move to and up slopes; white tails move down and away (75%, Lingle 2002, abstract). See [Escape terrain](#/s/escape-terrain/0).
- BC guidance for mule deer winter range timber: moderate to steep slopes, about 40 percent and more (85%, BC silviculture practices, 2004).
- Hunters avoid steep ground: in one Pennsylvania public forest study, hunters were 1.25 times more likely to use an area for every 10 degree drop in slope (85% for that forest). Steep ground near you may hold less pressured animals (Tip). See [Islands of habitat](#/s/habitat-islands/0).
```

## Slope and you: safety

```diagram rl-sa-avalanche
```

- Most avalanches start on 30 to 45 degree slopes (85%).
- Red on the Hunt Map means avalanche terrain in snow (99%, app).

```more steep ground safety
- Avalanche Canada: the vast majority of avalanches start on slopes of 30 to 45 degrees; they can happen from about 25 to 60 degrees (85%, search preview).
- A slide that starts above can run far out onto flat ground below. Do not stop under a red slope after heavy snow (Tip, app note).
- The Hunt Map slope colours are not an avalanche forecast. Check Avalanche Canada before entering snowy mountains (99%, app note).
- Shooting on steep ground: hold for the uphill or downhill angle. See the steep stalk lessons in the species section (Tip).
- Packing meat: a 25 degree slope is hard work loaded. Plan the pack out before the shot (Tip).
- Wet grass, frost and pine needles on 20 degrees plus are slippery. Unload before steep scrambles (Tip).
```

## Grandpa's rule and common mistakes

```photo hab-grass-fir-edge The warm face feeds them, the cool face hides them.
```

> [!rule]
> Grandpa's rule (wisdom): Cold morning, look to the sun. Hot noon, look to the shade.

> [!mistake]
> - Mixing up percent and degrees.
> - Glassing the shady north face at sunrise in a cold snap.
> - Ignoring a red slope above you after fresh snow.

```more the mistakes in full
- Percent and degrees: 30 percent is only about 17 degrees. 30 degrees is 58 percent. Check which one your source uses.
- In a cold snap, deer move to warm aspects and sunny beds: glass the south and east faces first at dawn (Tip).
- Avalanche terrain is 30 to 45 degrees. In snow, stay off and out from below red slopes (Tip, app note).
```
