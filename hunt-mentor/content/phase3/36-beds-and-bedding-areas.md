> [!why]
> Deer spend most of the day bedded. Find the bedroom and you know where the day starts and ends.

- A bed tells you who, how big, how long, and which way it left.
- Read beds when the animals are out. Hunt the trails, not the bedroom (Tip).

```more what this session covers
- [Reading sign](#/s/sign/0) showed what a bed looks like. [Trails and funnels](#/s/funnels/0) showed the daily loop from bed to feed. This session goes deep on beds.
- You will learn: reading a single bed in 4 steps, where deer pick beds and why (research and lore), weather and beds, does versus bucks, elk and moose beds, bedding areas, how to find them by map and by glass, 3 ways to hunt them, and how not to ruin them.
- Most of this is about deer, the owner's first big game. Elk and moose are covered briefly.
```

## Read a bed

```diagram rs-bd-anatomy
```

- Flattened oval, knee marks, hair, pellets, exit tracks (70%).
- North Dakota Game and Fish: beds "a couple feet long", oval (85%).

```more bed sizes in full
- A deer bed is an oval of flattened grass, duff or snow about 0.9 to 1.2 m long, often with pellets and a few hairs (70%, from [Reading sign](#/s/sign/0)).
- North Dakota Game and Fish: "Deer beds are a couple feet long and generally an oval shape. There may be several in one location. In vegetation, they are recognized by flattened grass. In snow, they may be recognized by melted snow and ice or exposed earth" (85%). A couple of feet is about 0.6 m: a curled up deer or a fawn makes a smaller bed than a stretched out adult (Tip).
- Elk beds: about 1.2 to 1.5 m, in groups (65%). A Roosevelt elk bed may run up to about 1.8 to 2.4 m long (60%, general website).
- Moose beds: bigger again, often alone, in willow or timber near water (60%). Exact size: VERIFY.
```

## Read a bed in 4 steps

```steps Read a bed in 4 steps
rs-bd-1 | Measure it: deer, elk, moose, fawn?
rs-bd-2 | Check the hair: colour, how much.
rs-bd-3 | Pellets and urine: stood and stretched?
rs-bd-4 | Facing and exit: walked or bounded?
```

- Bounds leaving the bed: it was spooked, maybe by you (80%).

```more the 4 steps in full
1. **Size.** Compare with the table above. A small bed beside a big one: doe and fawn (Tip).
2. **Hair.** Grey brown hollow hair: deer. Long coarse black: bear. Lots of hair: it lay a long time or it is moulting (Tip).
3. **Pellets and urine.** Deer often stand, stretch, urinate and drop pellets when they get up (Tip). Lore says a buck urinates in the middle of the bed and a doe behind it (50%: not proven, VERIFY before you trust it).
4. **Facing and exit.** The body shape shows where the head lay. Walking tracks out: it left on its own to feed. Stot or gallop groups out: something spooked it (80%). See [Measuring tracks](#/s/rs-track-measuring/0).
- Warm bed test: hand in the bed. Warm means minutes (Tip).
```

## Where deer pick beds: the research

| Study | Finding | Certainty |
|---|---|---|
| Utah mule deer, 1986 | Bed under or near conifers; less visibility on north and west sides | 75% |
| Forest day beds, 2004 | Site temperature and canopy closure mattered most | 75% |
| Arizona desert mule deer | Most thermal cover at beds in summer | 75% |

- Beds are about comfort and safety: temperature and cover (75%).

```more the studies, quoted
- Great Basin Naturalist (1986), Rocky Mountain mule deer: "Deer appeared to prefer bedding under or near conifers, and visibility was lower on the north and west sides of deer beds in both community types than on the south and east sides" (75%, abstract via search preview).
- Germaine and others (2004), mule deer summer day beds: "Site temperature and canopy closure were the most influential attributes in bed site selection" (75%, abstract via search preview).
- Arizona study, desert mule deer: thermal cover at beds was highest in summer, and thermal cover helps "thermoregulation, escape, and fawn protection" (75%, abstract via search preview).
- White tailed deer in winter in central Ontario were studied for bed site choice too (Armstrong and others, 1983) (70%, title via search preview).
- What it means for you: in October heat, check shady conifer patches; on cold mornings, check sunny spots with cover behind them (Tip).
```

## The classic day bed

```diagram rs-bd-slope
```

- Hunter lore: a bench in the top third, near trees, wind behind, view below (60%).
- The deer smells what comes from above and sees what comes from below (60%).

```more lore and its limits
- Day beds sit where the animal can see below and smell what comes from above: benches, the top third of a slope, under a tree with a view (hunter lore) (60%, from [Reading sign](#/s/sign/0)).
- Deer often travel with the wind at their back or side so they can smell behind and look ahead (60%, from [Wind and thermals](#/s/wind-thermals/0)).
- Morning thermals rise uphill and evening thermals sink. A deer bedded high in the morning gets rising air from below, so it can smell you coming from below even though it is "looking" there (75% for thermals). See [Wind and thermals](#/s/wind-thermals/0).
- Lore is a starting point. Your own logged beds at Heffley Creek beat any rule (Tip).
```

## Eyes below, nose behind

```anim rs-bd-wind Its eyes cover below, its nose covers behind.
```

- Come in from the side or from above with the wind in your face (Tip).
- Approach from below only when thermals carry your scent away (Tip).

```more approach rules
- Never walk straight up at a bed from below in the morning: rising thermals push your scent to the deer (75% for thermals, Tip for tactic).
- In the evening, falling thermals: approaching from above sends scent down to it. Come across the slope (Tip).
- Steady crosswind is the easiest wind for a stalk (Tip). See [Moving quietly](#/s/still-hunting/0).
```

## Weather picks the bed

```diagram rs-bd-weather
```

- Temperature drives bed choice (75%).
- Wind and rain push deer into thick cover and lee slopes (Tip).

```more weather in full
- Hot sun: shade, north and east slopes, conifer patches, cool draws near water (Tip, supported by the temperature studies, 75%).
- Cold and clear: south and west slopes in the sun, with cover at their back (Tip).
- Strong wind: lee side of ridges, low draws, thick timber. Deer get nervous in wind because they cannot hear well (Tip).
- Rain or wet snow: thick conifers, under big Douglas fir (Tip).
- Late October around Heffley Creek can bring frost mornings and warm afternoons. Deer may bed in sun early and move to shade by midday (Tip).
```

## Doe beds or buck bed?

```diagram rs-bd-buck-doe
```

- Groups of small beds: does and fawns (Tip).
- One big bed alone on an edge: maybe a buck. Lore, not proof (60%).

```more buck bed lore
- A mature buck often beds alone in a bigger bed on an edge, with thick cover or a drop behind it for escape (hunter lore, 60%).
- Rub lines often lead toward buck bedding areas. See [Rubs and scrapes](#/s/rs-rubs-scrapes/0).
- Big beds can be big does. Use tracks, rubs and what you glass to confirm (Tip).
- A fawn bedded alone is not abandoned. The doe is nearby (85%, from the photo caption). Leave it.
```

## Elk and moose beds

```diagram rs-bd-elk-moose
```

- Elk: many beds together, near water and wallows (65%).
- Moose: often alone, willow or timber near water (60%).

```more elk and moose in full
- Elk beds are about 1.2 to 1.5 m and come in groups (65%, from [Reading sign](#/s/sign/0)).
- In late winter, moose "significantly chose areas with shallow snow for bed sites" (75%, Alces journal, Michigan winter bed sites, via search preview).
- Snow 45 to 50 cm deep makes elk move to shallower snow (70%, Rangelands journal via search preview).
- Most Region 3 moose and elk hunts are LEH (Limited Entry Hunting). See [The rut](#/s/the-rut/0) and the Rule Book.
```

## A bedding area, from above

```diagram rs-bd-area
```

- Many beds, trails between them, pellets of mixed age (Tip).
- A main trail out to feed: your ambush line (Tip).

```more what makes a bedding area
- Bedding areas have many beds, trails between them, and pellets of mixed age (Tip, from [Reading sign](#/s/sign/0)).
- Look for cover that hides a standing deer within about 20 to 30 m, next to food within a few hundred metres (Tip).
- "Bed in the trees, feed in the grass, you on the line" (Grandpa's rule from [Trails and funnels](#/s/funnels/0)).
- The daily loop: [Trails and funnels](#/s/funnels/0).
```

## Find bedding areas

```steps Find a bedding area in 4 steps
contours | On the map: benches and north slopes near feed.
anim:hf-glass-point | Glass at first light: watch where deer go.
photo:g-md-does-bedded | Glass midday for ears and antler tips.
rs-bd-area | Walk it once, wind right, when they are out.
```

- Watch deer walk to bed at sunrise, from far away (Tip).
- Glass for parts: an ear, an antler tip, a leg line (Tip).

```more finding in full
- Map: look for benches, saddles and the shady side of ridges within a few hundred metres of feed and water (Tip). See [Map scouting](#/s/map-scouting/0).
- Morning glass: at first light deer feed, then walk to bed. Mark where they disappear (Tip). See [Glassing](#/s/glassing/0).
- Midday glass: bedded deer show "just heads and ears" (from the photo caption). Look at shade under trees, slowly (Tip).
- Check on foot only once, ideally when deer are out feeding or after the season, with the wind right (Tip).
```

## 3 ways to hunt beds

```diagram rs-bd-options
```

- My lean for a beginner: option A, ambush the trail between bed and feed (Tip).

```more options with pros and cons
| Option | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|
| A: Ambush the trail at dawn or dusk | Low risk of spooking, safe shooting lanes | Deer may use another trail |
| B: Still hunt into the bedding area | Works in wind, rain or soft snow | Easy to bump deer; shots are quick |
| C: Spot and stalk a bedded buck | Open country, you see him first | Needs wind, cover and patience; he may stand and leave |

> [!lean]
> My lean: A first. Sit on the trail between the bedding cover and the feed, wind in your face, at first and last light. Try C only after you have glassed a buck bedded and have a safe stalk route and backstop.

- Shot at a bedded deer: only a clear, safe shot with a backstop. Many bedded deer show only head and neck. Wait for it to stand if unsure (Tip). See [Shot placement](#/s/shot-placement/0).
```

## Do not bust the bedroom

| Do | Do not |
|---|---|
| Check beds once, wind right | Walk through every week |
| Scout when deer are out | Scout at noon when they are in |
| Glass from far away | Sit 50 m away "to watch" |
| Leave quietly the way you came | Cut through the middle |

- Bumped deer often move to another bed or go nocturnal (Tip).

```more why it matters
- "Walking through a bedding area at noon to check for beds. You just evicted the deer" (common mistake from [Reading sign](#/s/sign/0)).
- Pressure pushes deer into thicker cover and later movement (Tip). See [Weather, moon and seasons](#/s/weather-moon/0).
```

## Warm bed, fresh bed

```photo g-md-buck-bedded Warm bed? Freeze. Look ahead and to the sides.
```

- Hand in the bed: warm means minutes (Tip).
- Freeze, glass ahead and to both sides before you move (Tip).

```more what to do next
- A warm bed means the animal left in the last few minutes, often because of you (Tip).
- Look for it standing behind cover 50 to 150 m away, often watching its back trail (Tip).
- In snow: a fresh bed has a dry crisp floor; an old bed melted and refroze into an icy bowl (65%). See [Aging tracks and sign](#/s/rs-track-aging/0).
- No shot? Mark the bed, note the time, back out. Come back on a better wind (Tip).
```

## Grandpa's rule and mistakes

```diagram rs-bd-slope
```

> [!rule]
> Grandpa's rule (wisdom, not law): Read the bedroom once. Hunt the hallway every day.

> [!mistake]
> - Walking the bedding cover at noon every weekend to "check it".
> - Approaching a hillside bed from below on a morning uphill thermal.
> - Calling every big bed a buck bed.
