> [!why]
> Most trails end with the animal watching you go by. Good trackers look ahead, match the pace, and know when to leave the trail.

```photo hab-deer-trail-snow One line of tracks, close together: walking. Follow it with your eyes up.
```

- Track with your eyes ahead, not at your feet (60%).
- Wind first: never walk your scent up its trail (Tip).

```more what this session covers
- [Snow tracking](#/s/snow-tracking/0) covered backtracking versus following in snow. This session is the full technique on any ground: picking a track, light, the track stick, losing and finding the trail, pace, wind, the hook before the bed, working as a pair, when to quit, and safety.
- Following a fresh trail with a rifle is hunting: legal hours, land status and all hunting rules apply (95%).
- Honest note: big woods trackers walk a buck all day and fail more often than they succeed (Tip, from [Snow tracking](#/s/snow-tracking/0)). Patience is the skill.
```

## Pick the right track

```diagram rs-tf-choose
```

- Fresh, the right animal, and legal light left (Tip).
- Skip meandering doe and fawn trails if you want a buck (60%).

```more choosing in full
- "A common mistake is following the first set of tracks you see. Usually, those belong to a doe and her fawns" (60%, MeatEater via search preview).
- Benoit family lore (Maine white tails): bucks worth following usually leave tracks at least 7.6 cm (3 inches) wide, the best near 8.3 cm (3 1/4 inches) (60%, Outdoor Life via search preview). A Region 3 mule deer buck is bigger bodied; build your own numbers (Tip).
- Aging the track: [Aging tracks and sign](#/s/rs-track-aging/0). Measuring: [Measuring tracks](#/s/rs-track-measuring/0).
- Check the season and your licence for that animal before you start (95%). See [BC rules](#/s/bc-rules/0).
```

## Read the track, hunt ahead

```anim rs-tf-lookahead Glance down at the track, then look ahead.
```

- Bend to deer eye level and watch the terrain ahead (60%).
- When it slows to feed, slow down a lot and scan every direction (60%).

```more looking ahead in full
- When following a buck track, put yourself "in the animal's hooves", bend to eye level, and look ahead while following (60%, Realtree via search preview).
- "Once he slows down and begins to feed, you need to slow down exponentially as well. Really start to look over the terrain ahead of you in every direction" (60%, MeatEater via search preview).
- Look ahead, not down. Every few steps stop and glass the cover ahead and above (Tip, from [Snow tracking](#/s/snow-tracking/0)).
- Rule of thumb: 3 seconds down for the next track, 10 seconds ahead (Tip).
- See [Glassing](#/s/glassing/0) and [Moving quietly](#/s/still-hunting/0).
```

## Use the light

```diagram rs-tf-light
```

- Get low and look toward the light: faint tracks show as shadows (65%).
- Early and late light is best for tracking (Tip).

```more light in full
- Trackers call it side heading: get low, eyes near the ground, facing the light, to see tracks and grit compressions on hard surfaces (65%, Wildwood Tracking via search preview).
- Be at "as low an angle to the tracks as possible" (65%, same source).
- Midday overhead sun flattens tracks. Walk so the trail is between you and the low sun (Tip).
- At home, practise with a flashlight held low across a dusty floor. Never use a light to hunt or track with a firearm: that is hunting with a light (95%). See [Blood trailing](#/s/blood-trailing/0).
```

## The track stick

```steps Use a track stick in 4 steps
rs-tf-stick-1 | Set bands at the step and the stride.
rs-tf-stick-2 | Tip on the last clear track, along the line.
rs-tf-stick-3 | Sweep a slow arc, look near the band.
rs-tf-stick-4 | Found it: flag it, move up, repeat.
```

- A walking stick or ski pole with 2 rubber bands or O rings (65%).

```more the track stick in full
- A common build is a dowel or old ski pole about 0.9 to 1.2 m (3 to 4 feet) long, with rubber O rings or bands as movable markers (65%, Bear Tracker and Wildwood Tracking via search preview).
- Set the first mark at stride length, heel to heel; a second mark can show print length to confirm you are on the right trail (65%).
- Place the tip over the last clear print and sweep in an arc while looking for the next track under the mark. Keep the stick above the ground so you do not disturb sign (65%).
- "Stride varies with terrain and fatigue", so reset the bands every so often (65%).
- Look for crushed needles, bent grass, moved pebbles. Mark found prints with tape so you can backtrack (65%).
- Measure the step and stride first: [Measuring tracks](#/s/rs-track-measuring/0).
```

## Lost it? Circle out

```anim rs-tf-circle Flag the last track. Circle 10 m. Then 20 m.
```

- Never wander ahead making new tracks over the trail (70%).
- The trail must cross your circle somewhere (Tip).

```more the circle in full
- Lost the line? Walk a slow circle 10 m out around the last clear print and find where the trail leaves the circle (Tip, from [Tracks](#/s/tracks/0)).
- Lost the blood? Go back to the last flag. Never wander ahead making a second set of tracks (70%, from [Blood trailing](#/s/blood-trailing/0)).
- Check the likely ways first: the game trail, the easy downhill, the gap in the brush (Tip).
- Two people: one stays on the last flag as a fixed point, the other circles (Tip).
```

## Match its pace

```diagram rs-tf-pace
```

- Travelling straight: you can move faster (Tip).
- Feeding, wandering, beds: it is near. Creep (60%).

```more pace in full
- "The trail tells you when you are close: feeding turns to walking, then a single line going uphill, then a bed" (hunter lore, 60%, from [Snow tracking](#/s/snow-tracking/0)).
- In crunchy snow, walk like a deer: a few quick steps and a stop, maybe a soft grunt or bleat (60%, Hal Blood in Outdoor Life via search preview). Check calling rules for your species and area first.
- Stot or gallop groups: it is alarmed. Give it time to settle, sometimes an hour (Tip).
- Gait reading: [Measuring tracks and reading gait patterns](#/s/rs-track-measuring/0).
```

## Wind on the trail

```anim sg-snow-loop Track turns downwind? Loop around it.
```

- Follow with the wind in your face or across you (Tip).
- Trail turns downwind: leave it and loop around (Tip).

```more wind in full
- If the trail turns downwind, loop around rather than walking your scent to the bed (Tip, from [Snow tracking](#/s/snow-tracking/0)).
- Thermals change through the day: morning air rises, evening air sinks (75%). Recheck the wind every few minutes with a puffer or thread (Tip). See [Wind and thermals](#/s/wind-thermals/0).
```

## The trail hooks: leave it

```diagram rs-tf-hook
```

- A buck may loop back and watch you walk past (60%).
- When the trail starts to hook, swing wide and glass the bed area (Tip).

```more hooks in full
- "You might cover five miles of broken terrain just to realize the buck you're following looped back and watched you walk past from a ridge" (60%, Realtree via search preview).
- Hunter lore says deer often hook back before bedding so they can watch or smell their back trail (Tip; not confirmed by a study in this research).
- Big woods trackers walk a buck track until he beds, then stalk the bed from above (Tip, from [Snow tracking](#/s/snow-tracking/0)).
- Beds in detail: [Beds and bedding areas](#/s/rs-beds/0).
```

## Patience: how far away is it?

| Track age | Animal likely | Your plan (Tip) |
|---|---|---|
| Minutes | Within sight or sound | Freeze, glass, wind check |
| A few hours | Bedded, often within 1 to 2 km | Slow follow, glass often |
| A day or two | Still in its home range | Backtrack to learn, not to catch |

- A white tailed buck's home range is often only about 26 to 31 km² (60%).

```more home range in full
- Benoit Tracking School: white tailed bucks "tend to have their own home territory... that usually isn't more than 10 or 12 square miles", so a track a couple of days old can still lead to a buck close by (60%, Outdoor Life via search preview). That is about 26 to 31 km².
- Mule deer home ranges in BC vary by season and herd. See [Mule deer](#/s/mule-deer/0) and [How animals move](#/s/migration/0).
- Backtrack to learn its life. Follow to catch it (Tip, from [Snow tracking](#/s/snow-tracking/0)).
```

## Two hunters, two jobs

```diagram rs-tf-pair
```

- Tracker reads the trail. Spotter watches ahead and to the sides (Tip).
- Swap every 30 minutes; eyes get tired (Tip).

```more working as a pair
- Agree on hand signals before you start: stop, animal, bear, come here (Tip).
- Only one person shoots, on a safe agreed angle. Know your zone of fire (95% for the safety principle). See [Firearm safety](#/s/firearm-safety/0).
- In bear country the spotter carries spray in hand (Tip). See [Predator sign and kill sites](#/s/rs-predator-sign/0).
```

## Keep going or turn back?

```diagram rs-tf-quit
```

- Any "no" means stop, flag it, and come back (Tip).
- Pin the last track. Tomorrow you can start there (Tip).

```more quitting in full
- Legal light: no hunting from 1 hour after sunset to 1 hour before sunrise (95%). Leave enough light to field dress and pack out (Tip).
- Weather: rain erases sign; fresh snow can restart the hunt (Tip).
- Land: the trail does not give you consent to cross a fence. The Trespass Act has no tracking exception (95%). See [Maps: land status](#/s/maps-land-status/0).
- Your legs: the walk back is as long as the walk in. Set a turnaround time (Tip).
```

## Stay found

```video
yt-hug-a-tree | Lost? Stop. Stay put. Shelter and signal.
```

- Pin the truck before you start. Record your track on the GPS (Tip).
- Tell someone your area and return time (Tip).

```more navigation in full
- Following a trail pulls your attention to the ground for hours. It is the classic way to get turned around (Tip).
- Check your map every 15 minutes: where am I, where is the truck, how long back? (Tip). See [Maps: navigation](#/s/maps-navigation/0).
- Leave a trip plan with someone at home (Tip). See [Bush safety](#/s/bush-safety/0).
```

## Grandpa's rule and mistakes

```photo hab-elk-trail-snow Follow the story, not the footprints.
```

> [!rule]
> Grandpa's rule (wisdom, not law): The tracks are behind the animal. Your eyes should be in front of it.

> [!mistake]
> - Staring at your feet and walking right past the bedded buck.
> - Following the trail straight downwind into its bed.
> - Wandering ahead when you lost the trail, and stamping out the next print.
