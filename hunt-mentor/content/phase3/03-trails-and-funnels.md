> [!why]
> Deer do not wander at random. The land squeezes them onto a few lines. Sit on one of those lines and the deer comes to you.

- A trail is where animals walk again and again. A funnel is where the land forces them onto one trail.
- This session: trails, funnels, pinch points, saddles, benches, edges, bedding versus feeding, and how to turn all that into a stand or a glassing point.
- Nearly all of this is hunter craft, not science. Treat it as a strong Tip and test it on your own ground.
- Wind and thermals decide where you sit. Read [Wind and thermals](#/s/wind-thermals) first.

## Reading a trail

- A game trail is a bare, packed line 15 to 30 cm wide through grass or duff, with hoof prints and pellets along it (65%).
- Cattle trails are wider, deeper and churned, with cow pies. In Region 3 grassland many trails are shared (Tip).
- Count the sign per 10 m of trail. Lots of fresh pellets and crisp prints: a highway. Old faded sign: a back road (Tip).
- A trail that splits into many faint lines is entering a feeding or bedding area. A trail that gathers many lines into one is heading for a crossing (Tip).
- Deer trails run on the easiest line: along a bench, round the head of a draw, through the low spot in a ridge (65%).
- Follow a trail far enough to answer two questions: where does it come from and where does it go (Tip).

## Funnels and pinch points


- A **funnel** is any feature that narrows the possible routes: a strip of timber between two openings, a creek bottom, a bench, a cut block edge (65%).
- A **pinch point** is the narrowest spot in a funnel: a creek crossing, a gap in a cliff band, an open gate or a downed fence, the inside corner of a field (65%).
- Saddles: the low dip between two high points on a ridge. Deer cross ridges here, hidden from the skyline and using the least effort (65%).
- Benches: flat shelves on a hillside. Deer walk them and bed on them (65%).
- Edges: where two cover types meet, like timber against grassland or an old burn against green forest. Deer feed on the edge and bed just inside (65%).
- Water crossings: deer cross creeks where banks are low and gravel is firm. One crossing may serve a whole slope (Tip).
- In the [[Rut]], bucks cruise funnels between doe groups. A saddle between two bedding ridges is a November classic (60%).

## Bedding versus feeding

- Feeding areas are open: grassland, cut blocks, hayfields, burns with new shrubs, a south slope with saskatoon and bitterbrush (75%).
- Bedding areas are hidden: timber on a north slope, a bench with a view below, the top third of a ridge, thick regrowth (70%).
- Deer feed in the open at dusk and dawn, then move to bed as the light grows (85%). Morning trails run uphill, evening trails run downhill (Tip).
- Mule deer near Heffley Creek: grassland and open Douglas fir benches to feed, timber and broken rock above to bed (75%).
- White tails: hayfields and cottonwood bottoms to feed, thick brush and willows to bed (80%).
- Never hunt inside the bedding area. Hunt the line between bed and feed: closer to the bed in the morning, closer to the feed in the evening (hunter lore) (60%).

## Picking a stand or glassing point

1. Find the line: a trail or funnel between a known bed and a known feed (Tip).
2. Check the wind and the thermal for the hour you will sit. Morning: sit above or beside the line. Evening: sit below or beside it (75%).
3. Pick a spot 100 to 200 m off the line with a long view and a solid rest for the rifle. Past 200 m you are a watcher, not a shooter (Tip). See [Glassing](#/s/glassing).
4. Check the [[Backstop]] behind every lane you might shoot. Earth, not sky (Tip).
5. Plan a quiet approach that does not cross the trail, the feed or the bed. Come in the back way (Tip). See [Moving quietly](#/s/still-hunting).
6. Pick a B spot for the opposite wind. Never force A (Tip).
7. Mark it on your map and note where the sun will rise. A low sun in your face kills glassing (Tip).

> [!lean]
> For your first October trips, pick a glassing point above a grassland edge with timber behind you. Sit from 45 minutes before legal light. Learn the lines first, then move closer.

## Scouting on the map

- Use a topographic map or iMapBC, the province's online map, to find saddles, benches and creek crossings before you walk (80%).
- Contour lines close together: steep. A ridge line with a dip in it: a saddle. A wide gap between lines on a hillside: a bench (85%).
- Satellite view shows cut blocks, burns, hayfields and timber edges. Mark each, then mark the narrowest strip of cover between them (Tip).
- Walk the ground in daylight, at midday when deer are bedded, and from downwind of the bedding (Tip).
- Trail cameras work on funnels. A wireless camera is illegal for hunting from 1 August to 10 December except in Region 1 (95%). Use a memory card camera (85%, interpretation).
- Drones are illegal on a hunting expedition (95%).

## Rules that touch this session

> [!warn]
> Study aid only. The official regulations are the law.

- You may not hunt on cultivated land, or on Crown land under a grazing lease while livestock are on it, without the consent of the owner, lessee or occupant (95%). Synopsis page 12, item 53.
- It is unlawful to use a wireless camera for the purposes of hunting from 1 August to 10 December, except in Region 1 (99%). Synopsis page 11, item 30 (2 October 2026 edition).
- It is unlawful to operate or possess a drone on a hunting or trapping expedition (99%). Synopsis page 11, item 36 (2 October 2026 edition).
- Region 3: all motor vehicles except snowmobiles are prohibited above 1,700 m elevation except on existing roads and trails (95%). Region 3 synopsis page 33. Many good saddles are above that line; walk in.
- Ask yourself where the deer will fall. A trail on the far side of a creek can mean a wet pack out (Tip).

> [!rule]
> Find the bed, find the feed, then sit on the line between them with the wind in your face. Everything else is detail.

> [!mistake]
> - Sitting right on the trail. Your scent and your shape are on the line the deer must use.
> - Walking through the feeding field at dusk to reach the stand. Every deer saw you.
> - Picking a stand for the view and ignoring the wind.

> [!field]
> On a scouting day: walk one trail from an open feeding area uphill until it hits timber. Mark where it splits, every pinch point and every crossing. Pick one glassing point for a morning west wind and one for an evening east wind.
