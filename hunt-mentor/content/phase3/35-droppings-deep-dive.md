## Safety first

```diagram rs-dr-safety
```

- Gloves on. Open droppings with a stick (80%).
- Wash with soap before you eat (85%).

```more why canid scat matters
- *Echinococcus multilocularis* is a tapeworm that lives in wolves, coyotes and foxes and sheds eggs in their feces. In people it causes alveolar hydatid disease, "a deadly infection in people and dogs" (85%, Western College of Veterinary Medicine, University of Saskatchewan, 2015).
- BC's first known case was a dog near Quesnel in 2009. The parasite was later found in coyotes and foxes there, and surveys of hunter and trapper samples found it in new areas of BC (85%, same source).
- People get it by "accidentally swallowing tapeworm eggs from contaminated food or water, or from handling infected animals" (85%, Alberta Health, MyHealth Alberta).
- Alberta Health advises "disposable gloves if you are handling a coyote, fox, or other wild animal (dead or alive)" and washing hands with soap and water before touching food (85%).
- Risk to people is "very low" and "it's rare that people get it" (85%, Alberta Health). Low risk, easy prevention: gloves, stick, soap.
- Wash wild berries you pick on a hunt (85%, Alberta Health).
- Keep your dog from rolling in or eating wild scat (85%).
```

## Read a dropping in 4 steps

```steps Read a dropping in 4 steps
rs-dr-1 | Shape: pellets, rope, segments or pile?
rs-dr-2 | Measure the diameter, then the length.
rs-dr-3 | Open it with a stick. Read the menu.
rs-dr-4 | Note where it lies: trail, bed, roost, scrape.
```

- Then age it (Tip). See [Aging tracks and sign](#/s/rs-track-aging/0).

```more the 4 steps in full
1. **Shape.** Pellets (hoofed animals, hares, rabbits, porcupines). Rope with tapered ends (dog family). Segmented tube with blunt ends (cat family). Soft pile (bear, or a grazer on green feed) (75%, Washington Department of Fish and Wildlife, North Dakota Game and Fish).
2. **Size.** Diameter is steadier than length, because length depends on how it broke (Tip). Write it in centimetres.
3. **Contents.** Hair, bone chips, seeds, berry skins, insect parts, grass, wood fibre (80%).
4. **Place.** Middle of a trail, beside a bed, under a roost, in a scrape, on a rock (Tip).
5. **Age.** Moisture, shine, crust, mould (60%).
```

## Shape families

```diagram rs-dr-1
```

| Shape | Family | Certainty |
|---|---|---|
| Pellets | Deer, elk, moose, hare, rabbit, porcupine | 80% |
| Tapered rope | Coyote, wolf, fox, dog | 75% |
| Segmented, blunt | Cougar, bobcat, lynx | 75% |
| Big pile, variable | Bear | 75% |

- Bears "seldom, if ever, cover" their scat (80%).

```more shape families, quoted
- Bears: "Scats are highly variable... may be large and tubular in shape with relatively blunt ends or they may be relatively small and round/oval... Bears often deposit scats in large piles and seldom, if ever, cover them with debris" (85%, Washington Department of Fish and Wildlife, livestock investigation guide).
- Cats: "scats often appear segmented with blunt ends. Cats often scratch out areas and deposit scat in this spot then they may or may not cover scats" (85%, same source).
- Dogs: "scats often have a tapered end" (85%, same source).
- Coyote: "usually in a single cord and pointed at the ends... often contains hair... up to five inches long" (85%, North Dakota Game and Fish). That is up to about 13 cm.
```

## Pellets to scale

```diagram rs-dr-pellet-scale
```

- Deer: about 1.3 cm, pointed at one end (85%).
- Moose: about 1.9 cm across and 2.5 to 3.8 cm long (80%).

```more pellet details
| Animal | Size | Shape notes |
|---|---|---|
| Cottontail | Pea, about 0.6 cm (85%) | Round |
| Snowshoe hare | About 1.2 cm, slightly flat (70%) | Light brown, sawdust inside (70%) |
| Porcupine | Just over 0.6 cm across, up to 2.5 cm long (85%) | Light brown, slightly pointed, curved, big piles at dens and feeding trees (85%) |
| Deer | About 1.3 cm long (85%) | "Pointed on one end and slightly concave on the other" (85%) |
| Elk | About 1.3 to 2.5 cm long (65%) | Rounder, often dimpled on one end (65%) |
| Moose | About 1.9 cm across, 2.5 to 3.8 cm long (80%) | Oblong, rounded both ends, in piles (80%) |

- Sources: North Dakota Game and Fish (deer, moose, cottontail, porcupine), converted from inches (85%); hunting sources for elk (65%); [Reading sign](#/s/sign/0) for hare (70%).
- Big deer and small elk overlap. Use the tracks beside the pile (Tip).
- Mule deer and white tail pellets look the same (60%).
```

## Diet changes the shape

```diagram rs-dr-season
```

- Green summer feed: soft clumps and patties (70%).
- Woody fall and winter feed: hard separate pellets (70%).

```more seasons in full
- Deer on green feed drop soft clumps; in fall and winter, firm separate pellets (70%, from [Reading sign](#/s/sign/0)).
- Moose: dry distinct pellets in winter, softer clumps or patties in summer (65%). Winter moose pellets look like pressed sawdust (65%).
- Elk on lush grass drop patties like a small cow pie (65%).
- In October in the Thompson you will see both: patties near green irrigated hay or riparian grass, pellets on dry slopes (Tip).
- Cattle pies are much bigger and flatter than any elk patty. Region 3 grasslands have lots of cattle (Tip).
```

## Bears: the menu on the trail

```gallery
hab-bear-scat-berries | Berry scat. Pen for scale.
hab-bear-scat-okanagan | Seeds and skins on a trail.
```

- Tube about 4 to 6 cm across, or a loose pile in berry season (65%).
- Fresh berry scat on your trail: make noise (Tip).

```more bear scat in full
- Black bear: tube about 4 to 6 cm across and 13 to 30 cm long, often a loose pile in berry season (65%, from [Reading sign](#/s/sign/0)).
- Grizzly: the same but bigger, often over 5 cm across, with roots and meat (60%).
- Contents follow the season: grass and green plants in spring, berries in late summer and fall, plus insects, roots, meat and hair (70%).
- Bears often deposit scat in large piles and do not cover it (85%, Washington Department of Fish and Wildlife).
- Fresh bear scat, overturned logs and dug up anthills are "fresh signs that indicate recent bear activity" (85%, Alberta Environment, Be BearSmart for hunters).
- See the [berry calendar](#/s/food-water-cover/0) and [Bush safety](#/s/bush-safety/0).
```

## Cougar: blunt and buried

```diagram rs-dr-cougar
```

- Segmented, blunt ends, often in a scratched scrape (75%).
- Deer hair and bone chips inside (65%).

```more cougar scat in full
- Cougar: segmented tube about 2.5 to 4 cm across and 10 to 15 cm long, blunt ends (65%, from [Reading sign](#/s/sign/0)).
- "Cats often scratch out areas and deposit scat in this spot then they may or may not cover scats with ground debris. Scats may contain large amounts of hair and/or bone. Cougar scats will often be larger than bobcat" (85%, Washington Department of Fish and Wildlife).
- Cougar scrapes: a small pile of needles or dirt, scraped back with the hind feet, on a ridge trail (65%).
- Cougar sign means deer country. It also means a kill may be near. See [Predator sign and kill sites](#/s/rs-predator-sign/0).
```

## Wolf, coyote or dog?

```diagram rs-dr-canid
```

- Thumb wide and full of deer hair: wolf (65%).
- Finger wide, twisted, mouse hair: coyote (65%).

```more canid scat in full
| Animal | Diameter | Contents |
|---|---|---|
| Coyote | Under about 2.5 cm (65%) | Mouse, vole and hare hair, berries, small bones (70%) |
| Wolf | Over about 2.5 cm, up to 4 cm (65%) | Deer, elk or moose hair and bone (70%) |
| Dog | Any size | Often uniform, kibble, little hair (Tip) |

- Dog family scat often tapers at the ends and twists like rope (75%).
- Coyote scat "contains hair, which is more identifiable as the scat ages" (85%, North Dakota Game and Fish).
- Canids leave scat in the middle of trails and at junctions as a marker (Tip).
- Measure at the widest point. One wide wolf piece and one thin coyote piece can overlap; look at several (Tip).
- Gloves: this is the tapeworm group (85%).
```

## Small mammals

| Animal | Dropping | Where |
|---|---|---|
| Snowshoe hare | Flat ball 1.2 cm, sawdust inside (70%) | Runways in brush |
| Cottontail | Pea size (85%) | Brushy cover |
| Porcupine | Curved pellets, piles (85%) | Den mouths, under feeding trees |
| Red squirrel | Not scat: cone piles (middens) (Tip) | Under conifers |

- Hare pellets in piles mean it fed there a long time (65%).

```more small mammal details
- Hares drop pellets singly; piles only where they fed for a long time (65%, from [Reading sign](#/s/sign/0)).
- Hare twigs are cut clean at 45 degrees (75%). See [Reading sign](#/s/sign/0).
- Porcupine scat is "often found in large piles at the mouth of an underground den or under a tree in which it has been feeding". Bark stripped high in a tree is porcupine sign too (85%, North Dakota Game and Fish).
- Snowshoe hares are small game in BC with their own season. Check [BC rules](#/s/bc-rules/0) and the Rule Book before you hunt them.
```

## Bird droppings

```diagram rs-dr-bird
```

- White cap: a bird. Birds pass urine and feces together (80%).
- A pile of grouse droppings: a night roost (70%).

```more bird droppings in full
- Upland bird droppings "have a distinctive white coloration which is the result of dried urine products"; scat is often found "in large groups where birds have roosted overnight" (85%, North Dakota Game and Fish).
- Grouse: curved cylinders about 2 to 3 cm long, pencil thick, brown with a white tip, in piles at roosts (70%).
- Grouse also leave soft, dark cecal droppings, often at a different roost from the hard fibrous ones (65%, quoting Elbroch and Marks, Bird Tracks and Sign, via search preview).
- Canada goose droppings: green cylinders of grass (75%). Lots of fresh green droppings in a field means geese fed there recently (Tip). Geese drop about 6 times an hour while feeding (60%, Royal Society for the Protection of Birds (RSPB) blog via search preview).
- Owl pellets are not droppings. They are coughed up balls of hair and bones, light grey, under a favourite perch (85%, North Dakota Game and Fish).
- Bird sign deep dive: [Bird sign](#/s/rs-bird-sign/0).
```

## The contents are the menu

```diagram rs-dr-contents
```

- Berries in the scat: the berry patch is a feeding spot (Tip).
- Deer hair in predator scat: deer use this area (Tip).

```more how to use the menu
- Coyote scat full of rose hip seeds in October: rose thickets nearby. In the South Okanagan, rose thickets are quail cover too (Tip). See [Habitat tour: quail and grouse](#/s/habitat-upland/0).
- Bear scat full of fresh berries: a bear fed here in the last day. Go loud, spray on the belt (Tip).
- Wolf scat full of deer hair on a ridge trail: deer are using the drainage below (Tip).
- Pellets full of green fibre in October: deer are on green feed, often near water or irrigated fields (Tip).
```

## Scattered or piled?

```anim rs-dr-placement Scattered: walking. Piled: it stood or lay here.
```

- Scattered along a trail: moving (80%).
- A pile: it stood or bedded there (80%).

```more placement in full
- Deer scat "is generally found in piles where they have bedded or where they were standing. Scat may also be found deposited along a trail as the animal moved" (85%, North Dakota Game and Fish).
- Deer drop about 13 to 34 pellet groups a day, so piles are not a count of deer (70%, from [Reading sign](#/s/sign/0)).
- Biologists count pellet groups on set plots to compare deer use between areas (70%). You can do a rough version: count fresh groups per 100 m of trail on two ridges and compare (Tip).
- Grouse piles under a tree: a roost. In snow, a hole with droppings inside: a snow roost (70%).
- Cougar scat in a scrape on a ridge: a marking spot (65%).
```

## Grandpa's rule and mistakes

```photo hab-elk-pellets Measure it, open it, place it.
```

> [!rule]
> Grandpa's rule (wisdom, not law): The animal tells you its dinner, its address and its schedule. Read the whole letter.

> [!mistake]
> - Picking up coyote scat bare handed.
> - Calling elk from one pellet pile without looking at the tracks beside it.
> - Reading an owl pellet as a predator scat.
