> [!why]
> The same deer leaves a perfect print in mud, a hole in powder, and nothing on rock. Know the ground and you know what to trust.

- Every ground ages at its own speed (65%).

## Every ground, one table

```diagram rs-ss-grid
```

- Soft and damp grounds hold the best prints (65%).
- Track appearance varies hugely with ground and speed (85%).

```more the table in full
- "Depending on the substrate (snow, mud, dust, sand, etc.) and the speed the animal was moving, tracks may show great variability in their appearance" (85%, Maine Inland Fisheries and Wildlife pocket card).
- Mud: "great for seeing the clear details in tracks like toes, pad shape and toenails" and "typically the slowest aging substrate" (65%, Nature Mentor via search preview).
- Sand: greatest detail when wet; edges dry fast in sun, wind and rain (65%, Nature Mentor and Point Reyes Light via search preview).
- Dust: "great detail can be seen in the fine grain of dust, but suitable tracks are rare" (60%, search preview).
- Small animals need finer, crisper ground than big ones to leave readable prints (60%).
```

## Snow is not one ground

```diagram rs-ss-snow
```

- Wet snow captures a print well; dry powder does not (85%).
- Crust: few prints and loud walking (Tip).

```more snow types in full
- "Wet snow captures a paw print well, whereas dry powdery snow captures a less defined print pattern" (85%, New York State Department of Environmental Conservation, Conservationist, February 2015).
- "A thin layer of wet snow shows the best tracks, but as the snow ages, the edges of the track will soften. Hard, crusty, or wet snow, or strong wind, can make tracks nearly empty" (65%, search preview).
- Powder: you read depth, spacing and drag marks, not shape (Tip).
- Crust: animals may break through (deer) or walk on top (coyotes, hares). Your steps are loud; slow down or wait for a softer day (Tip).
- Snow depth and movement: deer struggle past about 30 cm, moose to about 40 cm, elk between (65% to 75%, from [Food, water and cover](#/s/food-water-cover/0)).
```

## Melt out: prints grow

```anim rs-ss-meltout Sun grows prints. Measure fresh ones.
```

- After sunny days a print can look much bigger and rounder (65%).
- A melted out fawn print can fool you into "elk" (Tip).

```more melt out in full
- In snow "an older track will increase in size and round off" (65%, search preview); after a few days prints "can appear huge with melting" (60%).
- South facing slopes melt prints fastest; north slopes keep them crisp (Tip).
- Measure only crisp fresh prints, on the floor: [Measuring tracks](#/s/rs-track-measuring/0).
- Shape clues survive melt better than size: toe count, claws, pattern (Tip).
```

## Mud: detail and a trap

```diagram rs-ss-mud
```

- Water seeping into the floor and a shiny rim: fresh (Tip).
- Mud ages slowest: a crisp mud print can be days old (65%).

```more mud in full
- In mud, fresh means the walls are still shiny and wet (Tip, from [Tracks](#/s/tracks/0)).
- Morning frost freezes mud prints solid; they thaw and soften by afternoon (Tip).
- Mud splashed onto clean leaves or rocks beside a crossing: something passed recently (65%).
- Elk rub wallow mud on nearby trees (65%, from [Reading sign](#/s/sign/0)).
- Muddy creek crossings and pond edges in the Fraser Valley and Interior: check for webbed duck and goose tracks too (85% sizes). See [Bird sign](#/s/rs-bird-sign/0).
```

## Sand: wet tells, dry hides

```diagram rs-ss-sand
```

- Wet sand by water: clear shapes (65%).
- Dry sand: read the spacing and pattern, not the print (Tip).

```more sand in full
- Sand "holds tracks well", but "its edges dry out fast", so freshness checks look at whether the raised edges are still moist (65%, Point Reyes Light via search preview).
- Wind ripples fill dry sand prints within hours on an exposed bar (Tip).
- Lake beaches and river bars are classic places to learn tracks: walk them at dawn (Tip).
- In dry sand the gait still shows: [Measuring tracks and reading gait patterns](#/s/rs-track-measuring/0).
```

## Dust and dirt roads

```anim rs-ss-overlay On top came last: a print over a tire was made after the truck.
```

- Dust shows fine detail, until the wind blows (60%).
- A print on top of a tire tread: made after that vehicle passed (Tip).

```more dust and roads in full
- In fine dust a fresh print has a thin raised rim that collapses within hours (60%, from [Tracks](#/s/tracks/0)).
- The overlap rule: whatever mark lies on top was made last. A track printed over a tire tread came after the vehicle; a tread over a track came after the animal (Tip, simple logic).
- If you know when a vehicle passed (yours, last evening), the overlap brackets the age of the track (Tip).
- Dusty grassland roads near Kamloops: drive slowly at first light and look for fresh crossings, then come back on foot (Tip). Never shoot from or across a road; see the Rule Book.
- Dust bowls from grouse and quail: [Bird sign](#/s/rs-bird-sign/0).
```

## No prints? Read what moved

```diagram rs-ss-litter
```

- Leaves, duff and rock show disturbance, not prints (65%).
- Crouch low and look along the ground toward the light (65%).

```more litter, grass and rock in full
- Sign of passage in litter: "displaced vegetation or stones", "turned over leaves", "dew knocked off vegetation", broken cobwebs (65%, tracking glossary via search preview).
- Grass: bent stems lie the way the animal moved (65%, from [Tracks](#/s/tracks/0)). A dark line through dewy grass is a fresh trail (Tip). See [Aging tracks and sign](#/s/rs-track-aging/0).
- Rock: look for scuffs of soil carried onto clean rock, moss scraped off, pebbles knocked out of their beds (Tip).
- Use low side light: [Following a track](#/s/rs-track-following/0).
```

## Natural track traps

```diagram rs-ss-traps
```

- Creek crossings, road edges, fence lines and field edges collect tracks (Tip).
- Check them first on any new piece of ground (Tip).

```more traps in full
- Animals funnel through the easy places: low spots in fences, shallow creek crossings, gaps in brush. Those are where prints pile up (Tip). See [Trails and funnels](#/s/funnels/0).
- Fence crossings collect hair too (65%, from [Reading sign](#/s/sign/0)).
- Road edges: walk the soft shoulder of a quiet forest service road (FSR) at first light. Read which way crossings go and how fresh they are (Tip).
- Smooth a strip of dirt on a trail with your boot one evening and check it in the morning: a homemade track trap (Tip).
- Private land and consent rules still apply at fences (95%). See [Maps: land status](#/s/maps-land-status/0).
```

## Snow and ice safety

```diagram sg-avalanche
```

- Avalanche terrain starts around 30 degrees once there is enough snow (70%).
- November river and lake ice is not trustworthy (Tip).

```more safety in full
- Avalanche terrain starts around 30 degrees of slope once there is enough snow to slide. Open slopes, gullies and the run outs below them can slide in November (70%, from [Snow tracking](#/s/snow-tracking/0)).
- Check the Avalanche Canada forecast before snow country trips (Tip).
- Do not cross river or lake ice to save a kilometre (Tip). Cold water: [Water safety](#/s/water-safety/0).
- Set a turnaround time; leave a trip plan (Tip). See [Bush safety](#/s/bush-safety/0).
```

## Grandpa's rule and mistakes

```photo hab-elk-trail-snow Know the ground before you trust the track.
```

> [!rule]
> Grandpa's rule (wisdom, not law): Ask the ground how honest it is before you ask the track how old it is.

> [!mistake]
> - Measuring a melted out snow print and calling it an elk.
> - Calling a crisp mud print fresh because it looks perfect.
> - Giving up on leaf litter because there are no prints.
