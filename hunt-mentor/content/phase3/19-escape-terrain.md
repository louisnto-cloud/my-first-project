> [!why]
> Every animal has a plan for danger. Know where it will run and you can stalk smarter, shoot safer and find it after.

```anim rl-es-flight Mule deer go up into rough ground. White tails go down and away.
```

- Escape terrain is the ground an animal runs to when threatened (85%).
- Each species has its own: cliffs, rough slopes, thick cover, distance, water (80%).

```more what this lesson covers
- What escape terrain is for mule deer, white tails, elk, moose, sheep, goats, bears and birds.
- How to predict where an animal will run before you stalk.
- How hunting pressure pushes animals into escape terrain.
- Safety: shooting near cliffs and steep ground.
- Builds on [Slope and aspect](#/s/slope-aspect/0) and [Bedding terrain](#/s/bedding-cover/0).
```

## Escape terrain by species

| Animal | Runs to | Certainty |
|---|---|---|
| Mule deer | Up slopes into rough, broken ground; bunch up | 75% |
| White tailed deer | Down and away, fast, into thick cover | 75% |
| Elk | Distance and dark timber, far from roads | 65% |
| Moose | Thick timber, willow, sometimes water | Tip |
| Bighorn sheep | Cliffs and broken rock near grass | 85% |
| Mountain goat | Cliffs of 40 degrees or more | 90% |
| Black bear | Thick brush; can climb trees | 75% |
| Quail | Run to thorny brush, rarely 30 m from it | 75% |

```more sources per row
- Mule deer and white tails: when coyotes came, mule deer "moved to and up slopes", white tails "moved down and away from slopes"; mule deer often stand and bunch, white tails flee (75%, Lingle 2002, abstract via search preview). Stotting is thought to work best on uneven ground (70%, Dellinger 2019).
- Mule deer: "Areas of steep broken terrain can also be used as security habitat" (85%, BC habitat account).
- Elk: elk security area ideas (big blocks of cover far from open roads) are widely used but debated (60%). See [Hunting pressure](#/s/pressure-push/0).
- Bighorn: "best suited ... to rugged, mostly treeless terrain"; lambs born in "rugged lambing cliffs" (85%, Bighorn Sheep in BC, 2000).
- Goats: escape terrain is "generally steep slopes usually 40 degrees or more ... of shear or broken cliffs" (90%, BC Mountain Goat Management Plan, 2010).
- Quail: "seldom ventures more than 15 to 30 m from cover" (75%, California Department of Fish and Wildlife).
- Black bears climbing trees: general wildlife knowledge (75%); see the bear claw marks photo in [Reading sign](#/s/sign/0).
```

## Mule deer: up and rough

```photo g-md-bound A mule deer stotting: bounding with all four feet together.
```

- Mule deer use slopes and broken ground to escape (75%).
- They often stop and look back from a high spot (Tip).

```more mule deer escape in detail
- Stotting lets a mule deer bounce up steep, rocky, brushy ground faster than a dog can follow (70%).
- Mule deer standing high on slopes were less likely to be attacked by coyotes than those standing low (75%, Lingle 2002).
- A spooked mule deer often stops at the top or behind cover to look back. That pause is the reason to stay ready, but never take a running or rushed shot (Tip). See [Legal to shoot](#/s/legal-to-shoot/0).
- On the map, rough escape ground shows as orange, red and purple on Slope steepness, often with rock bands (Tip).
```

## White tails: down, away and thick

```photo g-wt-run White tails bound away long and low, tails up.
```

- White tails rely on early warning and speed (75%).
- They head for thick bottomland cover (80%).

```more white tail escape in detail
- White tails flee at a gallop on gentler ground and rely on detecting threats early (70%, Dellinger 2019 via search preview).
- In valleys they run for willow, cottonwood bottoms and brushy field edges (80%).
- A flagging white tail rarely stops in the open. Watch where it enters cover: that is the trail to remember (Tip).
```

## Sheep and goats: cliffs

```diagram rl-es-goat
```

- Goats rarely go more than 400 to 500 m from escape terrain (90%).
- Bighorns live where steep rock meets grass (85%).

```more sheep and goat escape terrain
- Mountain goats "are usually reluctant to venture more than 400 to 500 m from escape terrain, often less distance during winter" (90%, BC Mountain Goat Management Plan, 2010).
- Along river valleys, goats also use steep mud and clay banks as escape terrain (90%, same).
- Bighorn winter ranges are steep, south to southwest facing grasslands with "rugged breaks and terraces along river valleys, rocky outcrops" (85%, Bighorn Sheep in BC).
- Young sheep learn where escape terrain is by following older sheep (85%, same).
- Sheep and goat hunting has its own licences and rules: see [Mountain sheep](#/s/mountain-sheep/0) and [Mountain goat](#/s/mountain-goat/0).
```

## Elk and moose: distance and thick cover

```photo g-elk-herd Elk herds run far, often into dark timber.
```

- Pressured elk move far and into timber away from roads (65%).
- Moose stand in thick cover or move off steadily (Tip).

```more elk and moose in detail
- Elk security areas in US planning: big blocks of cover at least about 0.8 km (half a mile) from open roads; the exact numbers were never validated (60%, Hillis 1991 as cited and debated).
- Elk often cross a ridge and keep going: one spooked herd can leave a basin for days (Tip).
- Moose often stand and stare, then trot off into the thickest timber or willow (Tip). See [Moose](#/s/moose/0).
- See [Hunting pressure](#/s/pressure-push/0) for where pushed game goes.
```

## Birds: cover in a heartbeat

```photo hab-woods-rose-slope Thorny rose along a dry creek: quail run here.
```

- Quail run to thorny brush, then flush (75%).
- Grouse flush into trees or curl back past you (Tip).

```more upland escape
- California quail stay within 15 to 30 m of cover (75%). They run first, then flush in a burst (75%). See [Quail](#/s/upland-birds/0).
- Ruffed grouse often flush to put a tree trunk between you and them (Tip). See [Grouse](#/s/grouse/0).
- Chukar run uphill ahead of you, then flush back downhill: climb above them first (Tip, as in the upland lessons).
```

## Predict the exit before you stalk

```diagram rl-es-predict
```

- Before every stalk, ask: if it busts, where does it go? (Tip)
- Stalk so the wind and the exit both work for you (Tip).

```more how to use the prediction
- Mark the likely exits on the map: uphill to timber, over a saddle, down a draw (Tip).
- If you hunt with your brother, one of you can watch the main exit while the other moves (Tip). Agree on zones of fire first. See [Firearm safety](#/s/firearm-safety/0).
- Never shoot at an animal on the skyline or running toward rough ground you cannot see beyond (Tip).
- After a shot, a hit animal often runs toward its escape cover. Start your search there (Tip). See [After the shot](#/s/after-the-shot/0).
```

## Pressure pushes animals into escape terrain

```diagram hf-slope-profile
```

- Hunters favour flat ground near roads (85%).
- Steep and far is where pressured animals end up (Tip).

```more the evidence
- In a Pennsylvania public forest, hunters were about 3 times more likely to use an area for each km closer to a road, and 1.25 times more likely for every 10 degree drop in slope (85% for that forest).
- So steep, rough ground far from roads gets the least pressure (85% there, Tip for BC).
- More in [Hunting pressure](#/s/pressure-push/0) and [Islands of habitat](#/s/habitat-islands/0).
```

## Safety on escape terrain

```diagram sc-cliff-shot
```

- Never shoot an animal on a cliff edge or above a drop you cannot reach (Tip).
- Your own escape plan: no stalks onto ground you cannot climb back up (Tip).

```anim sc-steep-stalk Climb out of sight behind a rib of rock, then come over above it.
```

```more steep ground safety
- An animal shot on a cliff falls: the meat can be lost and the retrieval can kill you (Tip). Wait for it to move to safer ground.
- Unload before scrambling (Tip). See [Firearm safety](#/s/firearm-safety/0).
- Red and purple on Hunt Map Slope steepness mean 30 degrees and more; in snow, red is avalanche terrain (99%, app key).
- Tell someone your trip plan before steep country (Tip). See [Bush safety](#/s/bush-safety/0).
```

## Grandpa's rule and common mistakes

```photo mountain-goat-nanny-kid Cliffs are a goat's front door.
```

> [!rule]
> Grandpa's rule (wisdom): Before you take one step, know where the animal will go. Then make sure you are not standing in the doorway, and not shooting into it.

> [!mistake]
> - Stalking from the side the animal wants to run to.
> - Shooting an animal above a cliff.
> - Chasing a mule deer straight uphill.

```more the mistakes in full
- If your approach pushes the animal past you or over the skyline, you get no safe shot (Tip).
- Animals shot near cliffs fall and are lost or ruined (Tip).
- A spooked mule deer uphill is gone. Back out, let it settle, come back another day (Tip).
```

## Do this in the field

> [!field]
> For every animal you glass, say out loud: its exit, your approach, your backstop. Then decide to stalk or not.

```checklist rl-es-field
Mark rough escape ground on one face with Slope steepness
For each animal you glass, name its likely exit
Plan approaches that do not push animals to a cliff edge
Watch a spooked mule deer: note where it stops and looks back
Agree zones of fire before watching an exit with your brother
```

## Memory hooks

- **Mulie up, whitey out.**
- **Goats stay a short walk from the rock:** 400 to 500 m.
- **Quail: a stone's throw to thorns,** 15 to 30 m.
- **EXIT before entry:** know the exit before you start the stalk.
- **No shot over the edge.**

```more using the hooks
- "Mulie up, whitey out" covers most deer escapes you will see in the Thompson and the Fraser Valley (Tip).
```
