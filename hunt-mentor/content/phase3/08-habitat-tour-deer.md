> [!why]
> You cannot hunt deer until you can see deer country. Learn the look of the land, the plants and the sign, and every walk turns into scouting.

- This is a photo walk. Look first, then read the bullets under each set of photos.
- Three kinds of deer country near our bases: Kamloops grassland and fir edge, burns and cut blocks up the North Thompson, and the wet coast forest around Mission (Tip).
- Mule deer and white tails live in the Interior; the Columbian black tailed deer lives on the coast and in the Fraser Valley (85%).
- Plants here are the ones from [Food, water and cover](#/s/food-water-cover). Now you see them.
- Photos come from many places with the same plants. Captions say where (Tip).

## Kamloops grassland and fir edge

```gallery
hab-lac-du-bois-grassland | Lac du Bois grassland north of Kamloops in October. Open bunchgrass, few trees.
hab-grass-fir-edge | The edge: grass meets Douglas fir. Deer feed in the open and bed in the trees.
hab-lac-du-bois-potholes | Rolling grassland with small pothole lakes and fir patches on the ridges.
hab-kamloops-sagebrush | Sagebrush slope on the Mara trail, Kamloops. Grey shrubs, bare soil between.
```

- Look for the line where grass meets timber. That edge is where mule deer feed at dusk and dawn (Tip).
- Winter range is the steep south and west facing slopes with old Douglas fir, often below about 760 m north of Kamloops (85%).
- Draws and gullies hold shrubs, shade and the last green feed in a dry fall (80%).
- Lac du Bois itself is a no shooting area inside the City of Kamloops boundary (95%). It is a classroom, not a hunting spot (Tip).

## Dry slope shrubs

```gallery
hab-bitterbrush-slope | Bitterbrush in bloom along a dry pine slope, Nevada. Same shrub as the Okanagan.
hab-bitterbrush-leaf | Bitterbrush up close. Tiny wedge shaped leaves with 3 teeth at the tip.
hab-sagebrush-bush | Big sagebrush. Silver grey, smells strong when you crush a leaf.
hab-ceanothus-patch | Snowbrush on an open slope. Shiny leaves, white flower clusters in early summer.
hab-ceanothus-leaf | Snowbrush leaf: glossy, sticky, 3 strong veins from the base.
```

- Bitterbrush: grey green shrub on dry slopes, tiny 3 toothed leaves (75%). Key mule deer browse on dry grassland (80%).
- Sagebrush: deer eat little of it, but it marks the dry, low country deer use in winter (70%).
- Snowbrush (ceanothus) springs up after fire and logging and is good deer browse (75%). Source: E Flora BC and Parish and others, Plants of Southern Interior BC.
- Look for twigs that end in a torn, ragged tip. Deer have no top front teeth, so they tear (80%).

## Draw and creek shrubs

```gallery
hab-saskatoon-flowers | Saskatoon in flower, Manning Park BC. White starry flowers, oval toothed leaves.
hab-saskatoon-berries | Saskatoon berries forming in early summer on a dry slope.
hab-woods-rose-hips | Wild rose hips in fall. Red, oval, on thorny stems.
hab-snowberry | Snowberry. White berries that hang on into winter.
```

- Saskatoon: shrub 1 to 4 m, oval leaves toothed on the top half, purple berries in July (80%). Top deer browse all year (80%).
- Rose: thorny stems and red hips; hips and twigs are eaten in fall and winter (75%).
- Snowberry: low shrub, white berries, very common in Interior draws and fir forest (80%). Deer browse it lightly (70%).
- A draw with all three plus water is a deer kitchen. Glass it at first light (Tip).

## Burns, cut blocks and Douglas fir

```gallery
hab-north-thompson-burn | Burned slope above the North Thompson River near Barriere. New green feed grows under the snags.
hab-kinnikinnick | Kinnikinnick, a low mat with red berries on dry, rocky ground under pine and fir.
```

- After a fire or logging, shrubs and forbs sprout for years. Burns and cut blocks are feed (80%).
- Mule deer often stay high in September in subalpine meadows, old burns and cut blocks (75%).
- Douglas fir: soft flat needles, cones with 3 pointed bracts (85%). Winter food and thermal cover (85%).
- Kinnikinnick: evergreen mat, leathery spoon shaped leaves, red berries (80%). It tells you the ground is dry and open (Tip).

## Mission and the coast forest

```gallery
hab-salal | Salal, the shrub that covers the coast forest floor. Thick oval leaves.
hab-salmonberry | Salmonberry. Orange or red berries in early summer, wet ground.
hab-bracken | Bracken fern on a cut block edge.
hab-blackberry | Himalayan blackberry thicket. Cover for deer and bears on Fraser Valley edges.
```

- Black tailed deer fall and winter food: salal, red huckleberry, trailing blackberry, Douglas fir, western red cedar, deer fern and tree lichens (80%).
- Spring and summer: grasses, fireweed, salmonberry, willow and maple (80%).
- Thick second growth and blackberry tangles are cover; cut block and power line edges are feed (Tip).
- Winter pushes coast deer to low, south facing slopes and floodplains with little snow (80%).

## Deer tracks and trails

```gallery
hab-deer-track-dirt | Deer track near Wenatchee, Washington, in dry ground like the Okanagan. Two pointed toes.
hab-deer-track-foothills | Deer track in soft soil. The split heart shape points the way the deer walked.
hab-deer-trail-reeds | A deer trail pushed through grass. Packed soil and tracks in the middle.
hab-deer-trail-snow | Deer tracks on a snowy trail. One line, prints close together: walking.
```

- A deer track is a split heart, pointed end forward, about 5 to 9 cm long for an adult (80%).
- Trails run from beds to feed to water. Follow one and you learn the daily loop (Tip).
- Fresh: sharp edges, no debris in the print. Old: crumbled edges, leaves or rain marks inside (75%).
- In snow, deep drag marks between prints mean deep snow and a tired deer. Leave winter range deer alone (Tip).

## Deer pellets

```gallery
hab-deer-pellets-fresh | Fresh deer pellets: dark, shiny and moist.
hab-deer-pellets-old | Old deer pellets: dry, pale and crumbly.
```

- Deer pellets are about 1 to 1.5 cm long, oval, often with a point at one end (75%).
- Shiny and soft means hours old. Dull, hard and pale means days to weeks (70%).
- Many pellet piles in one area in late fall marks winter range or a feeding spot (65%).
- Clumped pellets mean soft green feed; separate pellets mean dry browse (70%).

> [!rule]
> Grandpa's rule (wisdom): Find the edge, find the water, find the shade. The deer are where all three touch.

> [!mistake]
> - Walking the open grass at noon. The deer are bedded in the trees beside it.
> - Calling every twig a browse sign. Check for the ragged deer tear, not the clean hare cut.
> - Learning plants from photos only. Touch, crush and smell them once in the field.

> [!field]
> Walk one draw from bottom to top. Find saskatoon, rose and snowberry, one deer track and one pellet pile. Photograph each with your hand for scale. Then find the nearest water and the nearest fir patch.
