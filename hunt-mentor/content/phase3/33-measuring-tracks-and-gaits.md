```more what this session covers
- [Tracks](#/s/tracks/0) taught shapes, rough sizes and the 5 gaits. This session goes deeper: how to measure like a biologist, and how to read speed, mood and body size from the trail.
- You will learn: length, width, step, stride, straddle, group and gap; front versus hind; the 3 ways a hind foot lands; walk, trot, lope, gallop, bound and stot patterns; the wolf or dog size rule; and how to log it.

```

## The 5 measurements

```steps Measure a trail in 5 steps
rs-ms-1 | Length: toe tip to heel. No claws.
rs-ms-2 | Width: widest point, on the floor.
rs-ms-3 | Step and stride: write which one.
rs-ms-4 | Straddle: outside edge to outside edge.
rs-ms-5 | Groups: measure the group and the gap.
```

- Measure 3 to 5 prints and average (75%).
- Claws register on and off. Leave them out of length (80%).

```more the 5 measurements in full
1. **Length:** from the tip of the furthest forward toe to the back of the heel pad, along the foot's own line (80%, Washington Department of Fish and Wildlife). On hoofs: tip of the hoof to the back of the heel. Dewclaws are noted separately, not added in (Tip).
2. **Width:** from the farthest outside point to the farthest inside point, at right angles to the length (80%, Washington Department of Fish and Wildlife).
3. **Step and stride:** a step is one print to the next print. A stride is the same foot to the same foot, which is 2 steps. This app uses stride = same foot to same foot, as in [Tracks](#/s/tracks/0). Some guides measure heel to heel, others toe to toe. Always measure like to like (70%).
4. **Straddle (trail width):** outside edge of the left prints to outside edge of the right prints, at right angles to the line of travel (75%).
5. **Group and gap:** when prints come in clusters (bound, lope, gallop, stot), measure the length of one cluster and the gap to the next (70%).

- "Measure several footprints and average the size if possible" (80%, Washington Department of Fish and Wildlife, quoting Halfpenny and Furman 2010).
- "It is best not to measure claws, as they sometimes register well, other times not at all" (80%, same source).
- Measure several strides, not one. Animals change pace on slopes and when tired (70%).
```

## Floor, not the rim

```diagram rs-ms-floor
```

- Mud and snow push the rim out, so the top looks bigger (75%).
- Measure the floor of the print (80%).

```more why the rim lies
- "Try to measure from the floor (bottom) of the track, not the top edge" (80%, Washington Department of Fish and Wildlife, livestock investigation guide).
- Soft snow, deep mud and loose sand all spread the top edge. A print in slush can look a third bigger than the foot that made it (Tip).
- Snow prints also grow as the sun melts them out (65%). See [Aging tracks and sign](#/s/rs-track-aging/0).
- Best habit: measure the cleanest print in the firmest ground you can find on the trail (Tip).
```

## Front or hind?

| Animal | Bigger foot | Clue |
|---|---|---|
| Deer, elk, moose | Front, slightly (80%) | Front hoof rounder, wider |
| Wolf, coyote, cougar | Front, slightly (80%) | Hind narrower |
| Black bear | Hind is longer (75%) | Hind looks like a flat human foot |
| Snowshoe hare | Hind, 2 to 4 times as long (80%) | Hind lands ahead of front |

- Measure the front print for ID tables (Tip).

```more front and hind details
- North Dakota Game and Fish lists the front track "slightly larger" for white tailed deer, mule deer, elk and moose (85%).
- "The dog and cat families both have front feet that are slightly larger than the rear" (80%, Washington Department of Fish and Wildlife).
- Black bear: North Dakota Game and Fish lists front about 15 cm long by 10 cm wide, rear about 18 cm by 13 cm (80%, converted from 6 by 4 and 7 by 5 inches).
- Hares: "the back feet are two to four times as long as the front and always land ahead of the front feet" (85%, New York State Department of Environmental Conservation, Conservationist, February 2015).
- In a direct register walk the hind lands on the front, so you measure a blend. Find a spot where they separate, often on a turn or a slope (Tip).
```

## Size table: hoofs

| Animal | Front length | Front width |
|---|---|---|
| White tailed deer | 3.5 to 10 cm (80%) | 1.3 to 6 cm (80%) |
| Mule deer | 5.7 to 10 cm (80%) | 4 to 7 cm (80%) |
| Elk | 7.6 to 12.7 cm (80%) | 6.4 to 11.4 cm (80%) |
| Moose | 11.4 to 17.8 cm (80%) | 9.5 to 15 cm (80%) |

- Deer and elk overlap at the top end. Look at shape too (70%).

```more where the numbers come from
- North Dakota Game and Fish, Tracks and Signs of North Dakota Wildlife (government brochure), converted from inches: white tail front 1 3/8 to 4 inches long, 1/2 to 2 3/8 wide; mule deer front 2 1/4 to 4 long, 1 5/8 to 2 3/4 wide; elk front 3 to 5 long, 2 1/2 to 4 1/2 wide; moose front 4 1/2 to 7 long, 3 3/4 to 6 wide (85% for the reading, 80% for use in BC).
- These ranges include fawns and calves. An adult deer is usually in the upper half (Tip).
- Maine Inland Fisheries and Wildlife pocket card gives an adult white tail at 6.4 to 7.6 cm (2 1/2 to 3 inches) and moose at 11.4 to 14 cm (85%).
- Elk tips are blunter and rounder; "adult bulls will have blunt hoof tips" for moose too (80%, North Dakota Game and Fish).
- Prints splay (toes spread) on slopes, in mud and when running, which makes them wider (80%, North Dakota Game and Fish). Measure a relaxed print on flat ground.
```

## Wolf or dog? Measure it

```diagram rs-ms-wolf-dog
```

- Front 10 cm or longer, or hind 9 cm or longer, no claws: likely wolf (80%).
- Big dog breeds overlap. Read the trail as well (80%).

```more the rule in full
- "Tracks were likely made by wolves if either of the following is true (82% of dogs have feet smaller than this): Hind foot length 3 1/2 inches or longer without claws; Front foot length 4 inches or longer without claws" (85%, Washington Department of Fish and Wildlife, citing Halfpenny and Furman 2010).
- In centimetres: hind 8.9 cm or more, front 10.2 cm or more (85%).
- Breeds in the wolf range: Irish wolfhounds, Akitas, Saint Bernards, Newfoundlands, Great Danes and mastiffs (85%, same source).
- "Gaits and track patterns around kills and carcasses are often confusing and not diagnostic" (85%, same source).
- Tracker lore: a wild canid walks a straight purposeful line; a dog wanders and visits everything (60%).
```

## Where the hind foot lands

```diagram rs-ms-register
```

- Direct register: hind in the front print. One print shows (85%).
- Overstep is faster than direct; understep is slower (75%).

```more register in full
- **Direct register:** "the hind foot lands in the same spot as the front foot, leaving only one print" (85%, New York State Department of Environmental Conservation).
- **Indirect register:** the hind lands near but not in the front print (80%).
- **Overstep:** hind lands past the front. **Understep:** hind lands short. Understep walks are probably slower than direct register walks, and both slower than overstep walks (70%, tracking literature via search preview).
- Deer and elk at a walk or trot usually put the hind hoof on or near the front hoof print on the same side (70%).
- Cats direct register; a fox walking directly registers (80%, New York State Department of Environmental Conservation).
- A direct register print can look like a double print with a small offset. Do not read it as two animals (Tip).
```

## The pattern families

```diagram rs-ms-gait-key
```

- Walk, trot, lope or gallop, bound. Each leaves its own shape (85%).
- Faster: longer stride, narrower straddle (85%).

```more definitions from a wildlife agency
| Gait | Definition | Trail |
|---|---|---|
| Walk | Each limb moves on its own, never all off the ground (85%) | Alternating prints, short stride, wide straddle (85%) |
| Trot | Diagonal legs move together (85%) | Longer stride, narrower straddle (85%) |
| Lope | 2 moments with all 4 feet in the air; a slow gallop (85%) | Groups of 4 on a slant (75%) |
| Gallop | Fastest gait, at least one moment with all feet off the ground (85%) | Groups of 4 in a line, long gaps (80%) |
| Bound or jump | Hind feet land and push off side by side (85%) | Hind pair lands ahead of the fronts (85%) |

- Source: New York State Department of Environmental Conservation, Wildlife Tracking Skills presentation (Matt Walter) and Winter Tracks (Conservationist, 2015) (85%).
- "Trotting creates a longer stride and a narrower trail width than walking" (85%, same source).
- "As speed increases, prints space farther apart" (85%, same source).
- Rabbits, squirrels, mice and rats are jumpers (85%).
- A side trot is a trot with the body angled, so front and hind prints land in pairs side by side. It is mostly a dog and fox gait (70%).
```

## Deer gaits in numbers

```diagram rs-ms-stride-chart
```

- Mule deer walking: about 38 to 64 cm (80%).
- Stot groups: about 2.4 to 4.6 m apart (80%).

```more the stride table and the catch
- North Dakota Game and Fish "walking stride" ranges, converted: white tail 33 to 66 cm; mule deer 38 to 64 cm; elk 46 to 89 cm; moose 71 to 112 cm; black bear 43 to 64 cm; coyote 34 to 43 cm; wolf 33 to 61 cm; cougar 48 to 81 cm; bobcat 15 to 36 cm (85% for the reading).
- The catch: those numbers look like print to next print (a step). The New York agency shows a white tail trail at about 84 cm (33 inches) same foot to same foot, with a straddle of about 13 cm (5 inches) (80%). My reading: North Dakota measured steps, New York measured strides (60%). Compare like with like.
- Mule deer stot: "tracks are separated by an 8 to 15 foot stride" (85%, North Dakota Game and Fish). That is about 2.4 to 4.6 m.
- White tailed deer prints may be angled 10 to 20 degrees outward from the line of travel (80%, New York State Department of Environmental Conservation).
- When a deer runs scared, dewclaws print behind and the toes spread (85%, New York Conservationist).
```

## Read the mood from the pattern

```anim rs-ms-gait-change Walk, stop, stot. The trail shows the moment it knew.
```

- Zig zag walk, browse nips: relaxed and feeding (Tip).
- A sudden change in pattern marks a moment. Look around it (Tip).

```more mood reading in full
| Pattern | Likely mood | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| Wandering walk, stops, browse | Feeding, relaxed (Tip) | Slow down. Bed may be close |
| Straight walk or trot on a trail | Travelling with purpose (65%) | Find where the trail goes: bed or feed |
| Feet together, then stot or gallop | Spooked (80%) | Look back: was it you? |
| Long trot, nose to ground line | Buck in the rut on a doe trail (Tip) | Rut lesson: [The rut](#/s/the-rut/0) |

- Feet bunched together at a stop, then a gallop: the animal stood and looked, then fled (Tip).
- Read 20 m before and after any change. The cause (your scent, a coyote, a vehicle) is usually there (Tip).
- Speed check: stride grows and straddle shrinks as it speeds up (85%, New York State Department of Environmental Conservation).
```

## Body size from the trail

| Clue | Tells you |
|---|---|
| Straddle | Body width and weight (70%) |
| Walking stride | Leg length (70%) |
| Print depth vs your boot | Weight (Tip) |
| Drag marks in snow | Short legs or deep snow (70%) |

- Wide straddle and deep prints: a heavy animal (Tip).

```more size reading and buck lore
- "A wide straddle often indicates a heavy bodied animal like a bear or a badger; a narrow straddle usually belongs to efficient walkers like foxes or coyotes" (65%, tracking guide via search preview).
- Depth test: step beside the print with full weight. A print deeper than your boot in the same ground came from an animal heavier than you (Tip).
- Buck or doe from one print: not reliable (60%). Tracker lore from the Benoit family (Maine white tails): bucks worth following usually leave prints at least 7.6 cm (3 inches) wide (60%, Outdoor Life).
- A wide straddle with toes pointed out and a heavy, dragging walk in snow is the classic big buck story. It is lore, not proof (60%).
```

## Record it the same way every time

- 8 lines per trail. Photo with your knife (Tip).
- Your own notes become your best size table (Tip).

```more why logging matters
- Record the ground: "sand, snow, soil, wet mud, stone", plus date, time, location, weather, and how clear the track was (70%, Jack Raven Bushcraft via search preview).
- Use the app [Journal](#/s/hunt-journal-guide/0). One line per fact.
```

## Grandpa's rule and mistakes

```photo hab-deer-track-foothills Measure the floor, then measure again.
```

> [!rule]
> Grandpa's rule (wisdom, not law): One print tells you who. Ten prints tell you how. Measure both.

> [!mistake]
> - Measuring the top rim in soft snow, then calling a doe a big buck.
> - Mixing up step and stride, so your numbers never match a table.
> - Naming a wolf from one big print without checking for a dog trail.

## Do this in the field

```diagram rs-ms-5
```

> [!field]
> Find one walking deer trail. Measure 3 prints, 3 strides and the straddle. Then find where it speeds up and measure again.

```more the drill step by step
1. Pick a trail in mud or fresh snow.
2. Measure 3 front prints on the floor. Average them.
3. Measure 3 strides, same foot to same foot. Average them.
4. Measure the straddle.
5. Walk the trail until the pattern changes. Measure the new stride and straddle.
6. Write: what changed, and why you think so. Photo both.
```

```more more hooks
- "Floor, not the rim." Say it every time you kneel.
- "Two steps make a stride."
- "Hare: hind feet first." The big feet land ahead.
- Number trick: a mule deer stot gap is about the length of your truck, 2.4 to 4.6 m (Tip).
```
