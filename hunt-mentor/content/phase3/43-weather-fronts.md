## What a front is

```diagram rq-wx-front-cross
```

- A [[Cold front]]: cold, heavy air pushes under warm air (80%).
- Before: south wind, falling pressure. After: northwest wind, colder, rising pressure (70%).

```more the front, in plain words
- A cold front is the edge of an advancing mass of cooler, denser air that replaces warmer air (80%, US National Weather Service glossary, via the Weather Prediction Center).
- Typical sequence in the Northern Hemisphere (70%, general meteorology, US National Weather Service forecast office page, search preview):
  - Before it: wind from the south or southwest, clouds thicken, pressure falls.
  - At it: a band of rain or snow showers, gusty wind, a quick wind shift.
  - After it: wind from the west or northwest, colder and drier air, pressure rises, skies clear.
- A warm front is the opposite: warm air rides up over cold air, with a long spell of light rain or drizzle ahead of it (70%, general meteorology).
- In the BC Interior, mountains bend the wind. The valley wind on your slope may not match the forecast arrow (Tip). Thermals still rule the first and last hours: see [Wind and thermals](#/s/wind-thermals/0).
- Forecast source: use the official Canadian forecast for Kamloops or the nearest station. This app does not repeat forecasts (Tip).
```

## Watch a front pass

```anim rq-wx-front-pass Storm: deer move less. Calm and colder after: glass.
```

- The wind swings from south to northwest as the front passes (70%).
- During the storm, deer moved less (80%).
- Glass the first calm hours after it (Tip, not proven).

```more what the animation shows, line by line
- The rain cloud crossing the hill is the front. The wind dial swings from south to northwest (70%, general meteorology).
- The deer beds in cover while the storm is on: in the Penn State study, collared does moved less during storms (80%).
- After the storm the deer feeds. That part is a hunter's tip, not a study result. No study found for this session measured movement right after a front (VERIFY if you see one).
- Your scent: the wind shift moves your scent cone too. A stand that was perfect at dawn can be wrong by noon after a front (Tip). Plan a second seat for the new wind.
```

## What the collars say about storms

```diagram rq-wx-storm-bars
```

- 30 winter storms, collared does: no real change before the storm (80%).
- They moved less during it (80%).

```more the Penn State storm study in full
- Penn State Deer Forest Study, "Spidey sense", read directly 2026-10-02 and rechecked in this session's notes: collared does through 30 winter storms, 2015 to 2017 (80%).
- Their words: "Data show no statistical or biological significance of oncoming winter storms on behavior or movement of our collared deer." (80%)
- The deer slowed during the storms, not before them (80%).
- Limits: white tailed does in Pennsylvania forest, winter storms. Bucks, mule deer, fall fronts and the BC Interior were not tested (Tip: treat it as a strong hint, not a law).
- Penn State preliminary notes: bucks moved less in rain than does (65%, preliminary blog notes).
- So the "feed before the front" rule is not supported by the best collar study found. It may still happen on your hill. Log it and see (Tip).
```

## Temperature: the factor that shows up

```diagram rq-wx-temp
```

- One Oklahoma collar study: temperature explained most of the change in movement (65%).
- South Carolina collars: weather effects were real but smaller than the daily rhythm (65%).

```more temperature studies and what to do with them
- Mississippi State team, Oklahoma collar study: temperature explained most of the change in deer movement; no clear barometric pattern (65%, search preview via Mossy Oak).
- Goethlich thesis, Auburn University, South Carolina GPS (Global Positioning System) collars: weather, temperature, wind speed, pressure and moon each affected activity in some seasons and hours, but "responses to abiotic factors were typically less pronounced than circadian fluctuations in activity" (65%, search preview). Plain English: the time of day matters more than the weather.
- Penn State October data: deer moved about 120 to 140 m an hour at dawn and dusk, under 50 m an hour in the day and about 60 m an hour at night (75%, Penn State Deer Forest blog, read directly). Dawn and dusk beat any weather effect.
- A hot October spell: deer bed in shade (north and east faces, timber) and move at the very edges of legal light (Tip).
- A cold snap after mild weather: hunters report more daylight feeding. No study found for the Interior; log it (Tip).
- Trend beats hour: compare today to the last 3 days, not to 6 am (Tip).
```

## Snow and cold move mule deer

```diagram hf-snow-depth
```

- Snow and cold trigger the fall move to [[Winter range]] (85%).
- Trouble near 30 cm of snow; deer leave slopes near 50 cm (75%).

```more snow, cold and migration timing in full
- Trigger: snow and cold, not the rut (85%, Monteith and others 2011, Ecosphere, via research notes).
- Wyoming GPS collars, 2016 to 2018: temperature and snowfall set migration timing in both sexes; the rifle season added only about 2% to the chance a doe migrated and under 0.01% for bucks (85%, Rodgers and others 2021, Journal of Wildlife Management, abstract read directly).
- BC deer account: deer "have difficulty moving through snow deeper than 30 cm" (85%). A UBC (University of British Columbia) thesis: deer leave slopes near 50 cm (80%). Use 30 to 50 cm as the range (75%).
- Southern Interior Mule Deer Project: most collared deer were back on winter range in October and November; one year nearly all by mid October (85%).
- So for a mid to late October hunt near Heffley Creek: the first real snow up high is the best weather news you can get (Tip). See [Migration routes in BC](#/s/bc-migration-routes/0).
```

## Ducks: freeze up moves them

```diagram rq-wx-wsi
```

- Cold days add up, ice locks the food, dabblers leave (75%).
- Interior freeze up ends most BC fall migration by late November (75%).

```more the weather severity index and BC timing
- Schummer and others 2010, Journal of Wildlife Management: in Missouri 1995 to 2005, a cumulative weather severity index best explained changes in mallard and dabbling duck numbers. It adds up mean temperature, the run of days at or below 0 °C, snow depth and the run of snow cover days (75%, abstract read directly).
- Why it works: cold costs energy; snow and ice hide food (75%, same study).
- BC: "The fall migration largely concluded by late November, coinciding with freeze-up in most of the province." (75%, Ducks Unlimited 2024 field report, quoted with its hyphen)
- Plateau lakes freeze first, valley rivers later. When the high potholes ice over, ducks pack onto open valley water (Tip, consistent with the freeze up pattern).
- Daily flights by weather: see [Waterfowl daily flights](#/s/waterfowl-daily-flights/0).
```

## Geese, pheasants, chukar and storms

```diagram rq-wx-birds
```

- Stormy days: geese flew more through the whole day (70%).
- Worse weather pushes pheasants deeper into cover (70%).
- Heavy snow pushes chukar lower and onto south slopes (75%).

```more the bird studies and sources
- Canada geese, Crab Orchard refuge, Illinois, about 40,000 birds over two winters: geese "usually fly to feeding areas in the early morning and late afternoons but undertake increased flight activity throughout the day when it is stormy" (70%, Raveling, Crews and Klimstra, Wilson Bulletin, search preview).
- Pheasants: Minnesota DNR (Department of Natural Resources): worse weather pushes birds deeper into cover (70%, read directly). Pheasants in BC: Okanagan and Georgia Depression, chiefly below 500 m (85%, BC Breeding Bird Atlas).
- Chukar: "moving from higher elevations to lower terrain during heavy snows", may move onto south facing slopes (75%, USDA Fire Effects Information System). In BC: "Prolonged cold temperatures plus deep snow" are the chief threat (80%, Demarchi thesis via research notes).
- Ruffed grouse dive into soft snow to roost (80%). See [Habitat tour: quail and grouse](#/s/habitat-upland/0).
- Upland daily patterns by terrain: [Upland birds' daily pattern](#/s/upland-daily-pattern/0).
```

## Read a forecast for hunting

```steps Read a forecast in 5 steps
rq-wx-step-1 | Check wind direction and speed first.
rq-wx-step-2 | Check the temperature trend over 3 days.
rq-wx-step-3 | Check rain, snow and the snow line.
rq-wx-step-4 | Find when the front passes.
rq-wx-step-5 | Pick spot A or B to fit.
```

- Wind first, always (Tip).
- Write the plan the night before (Tip).

```more the 5 steps in full
1. Wind: direction and speed for dawn, midday and dusk. Your whole approach depends on it (Tip). Thermals: up in the morning as the slope warms, down in the evening (80%).
2. Temperature trend: falling 3 days in a row, or a warm spell? Warm: shade and the edges of the day. Cold: sunny faces in the morning (Tip).
3. Rain or snow, and the freezing level or snow line. Fresh snow up high pushes mule deer down over days to weeks (85% for the trigger; timing varies).
4. Front timing: if rain or snow hits at 10 am, glass hard from first light to 9:30, sit tight in cover during, and glass the first calm after (Tip).
5. Spot A for the forecast wind, spot B for the other wind. Check both against closures and the [Region 3 Rule Book](#/s/rb-region-3/0) before you go.
- The night before checklist lives in [Weather, moon and what moves game](#/s/weather-moon/0).
```

## Plan by weather

| Forecast | Deer plan (Tip) | Bird plan (Tip) |
|---|---|---|
| Clear, calm, cold | Sunny east and south faces at dawn | Ducks fly early to fields |
| Warm spell | Shade, timber edges, last light | Midday is slow |
| Front coming | Glass hard before it | Geese may fly all day in it |
| Storm on | Sit, glass sheltered lee slopes | Ducks seek calm water |
| First snow up high | Lower slopes, south and west faces | Plateau ponds may ice |
| Freeze up | Winter range edges | Open river water, coast |

- Every cell is a tip built on this session's studies. Log what you see.

```more how each row was built
- Clear and cold: deer on sunny faces at dawn is hunter practice (Tip); dawn and dusk movement peaks are measured (75%).
- Storm on: deer moved less during storms (80%). Lee slopes for wind shelter is a tip.
- Geese flying all day in storms: Illinois study (70%).
- First snow and freeze up: mule deer trigger (85%) and duck freeze up (75%).
- Ducks seeking calm water in wind is a waterfowler's tip, not a study result here (Tip).
```

## Common mistakes

```diagram hf-evidence-bars
```

> [!mistake]
> - Staying home because the barometer "looks wrong".
> - Keeping the dawn seat after a front swung the wind.
> - Hunting high ground after the first big snow.

```more the mistakes explained
- The barometer: weak and mixed evidence. Losing a free day to it costs more than any pressure effect found (Tip).
- The wind swing: a front can turn a south wind into a northwest wind in minutes (70%). Your scent goes with it (Tip).
- After snow up high, many mule deer start down toward winter range (85%). The high bench can go empty (Tip).
```

## Memory hooks

```diagram rq-wx-front-cross
```

- **"South wind before, northwest wind after, pressure low then higher."** The front passes.
- **"Storm: still. Calm: fill."** Deer moved less in storms (80%); glass the calm (Tip).
- **30 and 50:** snow trouble starts near 30 cm, deer leave near 50 cm (75%).
