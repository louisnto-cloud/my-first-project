## The bands near Kamloops

```diagram rl-eb-bands
```

- Low: bunchgrass. Middle: pine and Douglas fir. High: spruce and subalpine fir. Top: alpine (90%).
- Bands overlap and shift with aspect and rain (85%).

```more the zones and their heights
| Zone (BEC, Biogeoclimatic Ecosystem Classification) | Typical height in the southern Interior |
|---|---|
| BG, Bunchgrass | Valley bottoms up to about 700 to 1,000 m |
| PP, Ponderosa Pine | About 335 to 900 m |
| IDF, Interior Douglas fir | Lower edge 350 to 600 m, upper edge 900 to 1,450 m |
| MS, Montane Spruce | About 1,100 to 1,700 m |
| ESSF, Engelmann Spruce Subalpine Fir | About 1,200 to 2,300 m |
| Alpine | Above about 1,650 to 2,250 m |

- Source: Meidinger and Pojar 1991, Ecosystems of British Columbia, as logged in the Hunt Mentor research notes (90%).
- The ranges overlap because warm south faces push each band higher and cool north faces pull it lower (80%).
- Near the coast, the bands are different: Coastal Western Hemlock from sea level to about 900 to 1,050 m (90%).
```

## Air cools as you climb

```diagram rl-eb-lapse
```

- About 6.5°C cooler per 1,000 m, on an average day (80%).
- Rain at the truck can be snow on the ridge (Tip).

```more lapse rate details
- In the standard atmosphere, air cools about 6.5°C for every 1,000 m you climb (80%, weather teaching sources). Real days vary a lot.
- So a 1,000 m climb from the valley to a ridge can turn 4°C rain into wet snow (Tip).
- Pack for the top, not the truck. See [Layering](#/s/weather-moon/0) and the weather lessons (Tip).
- The temperatures in the drawing are an example day, not a forecast.
```

## Clear calm nights flip it

```diagram rl-eb-inversion
```

- Cold air drains down at night and pools in the valley (75%).
- Frost and fog sit low; the bench above can be warmer (75%).

```more inversions and cold air pools
- On clear, calm nights the ground loses heat fast and cold, heavy air slides down slopes into the valley (75%). Air higher up stays warmer: an inversion.
- Studies in western mountain valleys measured cold pools many degrees colder than the air above (75%).
- Animals may bed above the frost line on cold clear nights (Tip).
- The same downhill drain carries your scent down at dusk and before dawn. See [Wind and thermals](#/s/wind-thermals/0).
- Thompson and Okanagan valleys often hold fog or haze under an inversion in late fall (Tip). Glass from above the fog if you can.
```

## The snowline through the fall

```anim sg-mig-snowline Snow drops, deer drop.
```

- September to November, snow creeps down the mountains (85%).
- Deep snow pushes deer, then elk and moose, to lower ground (85%).

```more timing in the southern Interior
- BC mule deer study (BCWF, BC Wildlife Federation, Southern Interior Mule Deer Project): most deer return to winter range in October and November; in 2019, "by mid October ... nearly all the collared deer in each study area were back in their winter range" (85%).
- The trigger is snow and cold, not the calendar or the rut (85%, Monteith 2011, as logged).
- Moose "stay on high elevation ranges much longer than elk, deer or mountain sheep" (85%, BC moose account, 2000).
- No source found for the usual snowline height by month near Kamloops. Watch it yourself and log it in the Journal (VERIFY each year).
- See [Migration routes in BC](#/s/bc-migration-routes/0) and [Winter ranges](#/s/winter-range-legal/0).
```

## How deep is too deep

```diagram sg-snow-depth
```

- Deer struggle past about 25 to 30 cm, avoid over 50 cm (80%).
- Moose move freely to about 40 cm (70%).

```more snow depth numbers
- Deer spend the most energy in soft, crustless snow where they sink more than 25 cm (85%, BC habitat account citing Nyberg and Janz 1990).
- "If snow accumulations exceed 50 cm then an area is generally avoided" by mule deer (85%, same account).
- Moose move freely to about 40 cm, are restricted at 40 to 70 cm (70%). Elk sit between deer and moose (65%). As in [Food, water and cover](#/s/food-water-cover/0).
- Crusted snow that holds a deer is easier for it; crusted snow that breaks under a deer but holds a coyote is deadly for deer (Tip).
```

## The snowline bends with aspect

```diagram rl-eb-aspect-snow
```

- Snow melts first and lies thinnest on sunny south faces (85%).
- The first bare ground below new snow pulls deer (Tip).

```more why it bends
- Winter range for mule deer is on south and west aspects because "the snowpack is lower" there (85%, BC habitat account).
- After an early storm, look for deer on the sunny face just below the snow edge (Tip).
- North faces hold snow longer but their thick timber catches snow in the branches, so the ground under the trees can be shallower than in the open (85%). Deer use that in deep snow years.
```

## Who lives how high

```diagram rl-eb-who
```

- Quail and chukar stay low. Dusky grouse go high in fall (80%).
- Ruffed grouse live in the middle band (85%).

```more where each number comes from
- California quail: breeding mostly 250 to 500 m, usually below 750 m, some to 1,250 m (85%, BC Breeding Bird Atlas). They do not migrate (75%).
- Chukar: 195 to 610 m in a 1962 Thompson study; they move lower in heavy snow (80%, Demarchi 1962; 75%, US Fire Effects Information System).
- Bighorn sheep winter and spring ranges: 300 to 1,825 m (85%, Bighorn Sheep in BC).
- Mule deer winter range: below about 1,500 m in shallow and moderate snow zones, below 1,000 m in deep snow zones (85%, BC habitat account citing US Forest Service 1998).
- Ruffed grouse: most abundant 750 to 1,250 m (85%, BC Breeding Bird Atlas).
- Dusky grouse: "many observations in the 1,500 to 2,250 m range"; they move uphill to dense conifers in fall (85% and 70%).
- Moose and elk: no single height band; they follow food and snow (Tip). See the species lessons.
```

## Read the snowline from the truck

```diagram rl-eb-read-snow
```

- Find the snow edge on the mountain.
- Find the same spot on the Hunt Map and read the contour (Tip).

```steps Put a number on the snowline
rl-hm-spot | Centre the map on the snow edge: read the height.
rl-hm-3d | Tilt the map to match what you see.
rl-hm-profile | Draw a profile to see how far below it to hunt.
```

```more how to do it well
- Pick a landmark at the snow edge: a rock band, a cutblock corner, a meadow. Find it on the map (Tip).
- Put the map cross on it. The readout at the top left shows the ground height under the cross (99%, app).
- Write it in the Journal with the date and aspect. Over a season you will see the line march down (Tip).
- Hunt the band just below fresh snow on the warm faces in a cold snap (Tip).
```

## The law at 1,700 m

> [!warn]
> Study aid only. The official regulations are the law.

```diagram gr-1700
```

- Region 3: above 1,700 m, motor vehicles stay on existing roads and trails, snowmobiles excepted (99%).
- Region 8 has its own 1,700 m rule. Read it in the Rule Book.

```more the exact rule and where to read it
> [!law] Region 3, region wide
> "The operation of all motor vehicles, excluding snowmobiles is prohibited in region 3 above 1,700 m elevation, except on existing roads and trails."

- Carried in full with page numbers in [Rule Book: Region 3](#/s/rb-region-3/0) and [Rule Book: Region 8](#/s/rb-region-8/0) (99% as carried there).
- The Hunt Map readout tells you when you are near 1,700 m (99%, app). Use it before you leave a road with the ATV (All Terrain Vehicle).
```

## Grandpa's rule and common mistakes

```photo hab-subalpine-forest High country empties when the snow comes.
```

> [!rule]
> Grandpa's rule (wisdom): Hunt the edge of the snow, not the middle of it. The animals live where the white stops.

> [!mistake]
> - Hunting the high summer range after a big October storm.
> - Dressing for the valley and climbing into snow.
> - Driving off road above 1,700 m in Region 3.

```more the mistakes in full
- After the first big storm, much of the high summer range empties. Move down to the snow edge and the warm faces (Tip).
- Air cools about 6.5°C per 1,000 m: pack for the top (80%).
- Above 1,700 m in Region 3, vehicles stay on existing roads and trails (99%, Rule Book).
```

## Do this in the field

> [!field]
> Every trip, log the snowline height and which face it is on. Compare it with where you see deer.

```checklist rl-eb-field

Log the snowline height on the warm and the cool face

Note frost or fog in the valley at dawn
Check your height before leaving a road on the ATV
```

- **Snow drops, deer drop.**

- **25 to struggle, 50 to leave:** cm of snow for deer.
- **Clear and calm, the valley's the freezer.**
