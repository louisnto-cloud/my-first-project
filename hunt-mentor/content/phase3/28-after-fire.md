> [!why]
> BC burns a lot, and a burn changes month by month for 20 years. Know what each age of burn looks like and who feeds there, and a burned hillside becomes a map of where game will be.

- New green feed grows under the dead trees (85%).
- Each age of burn feeds different animals (70%).
- Burns are also dangerous ground (85%).

## A burn, year by year

```steps A burn from year 0 to 20
rp-burn-y0 | Year 0: black ground, ash, standing dead trees.
rp-burn-y1 | Year 1 to 2: grass, herbs and fireweed come up.
rp-burn-y3 | Year 3 to 5: shrubs and aspen suckers. Peak browse.
rp-burn-y8 | Year 6 to 10: young trees rise, snags crash down.
rp-burn-y15 | Year 10 to 20: dense young forest. Cover, less food.
```

- Black, then green, then shrubs, then thicket (75%).
- The best feed years are roughly 2 to 10 (65%).

```more the stages in detail
- Year 0: ash, black soil, standing dead trees (snags). Ash pits can stay hot long after the flames (85%, BC Wildfire Service).
- Years 1 to 2: grasses and herbs flush green. Seeds of snowbrush (ceanothus) and lodgepole pine germinate in response to fire (90%, BC restoration guidelines). Elk use of burned grassland went up in the first 1 to 2 years after prescribed burns in Montana (70%).
- Years 3 to 5: shrubs and sprouts. Trembling aspen regenerates abundantly from root suckers that quickly form a dense shrub layer (85%, E Flora BC). Willow, saskatoon and rose resprout (75%).
- Years 6 to 10: young lodgepole pine and aspen rise. Most fire killed snags break or fall in the first 15 years; lodgepole and ponderosa pine snags fall sooner than Douglas fir (65 to 70%, Pacific Northwest studies, search preview).
- Years 10 to 20: dense regeneration. In the MS (Montane spruce) zone, dense lodgepole regeneration thickets give moose and mule deer thermal and hiding cover (95%, zone book).
- In BC's structural stages, burned and cut areas pass through herb (stage 2) and shrub (stage 3) stages, usually under 20 years for herbs and under 40 years for tall shrubs, before the pole and sapling stage (90%).
```

## Who uses a burn, and when

```diagram rp-burn-who
```

- Bears and elk: early green years (70%).
- Deer and moose: shrub years (70%).
- Woodpeckers: dead trees first (90%).

```more the evidence, study by study
- Black bears forage burned areas in spring for succulent green plants (85%, BC Ministry of Environment, Black Bear in BC).
- Elk in sagebrush grassland (Montana): use rose in years 1 to 2 after prescribed burning, then fell back to pre burn levels over 3 to 10 years as forage quantity and protein dropped (70%, Van Dyke and Darragh 2007, search preview).
- Elk in Yellowstone used burned grassland more after the 1988 fires but avoided burned forest for the first 3 winters (65%, search preview).
- Mule deer in an Arizona ponderosa pine burn: use passed the unburned area after 2 years, tied to palatable plants, not total plant mass (65%, Kruse 1972, search preview).
- Moose in BC's Mount McAllister burn (26,500 ha, about 5 years old, north central BC): moose used low and medium severity patches, especially wet sites (75%, McNay and others 2021, Alces 57).
- Boreal moose in Alberta, Saskatchewan and northeast BC showed low use of burns up to 40 years old (65%, DeMars and others 2019, search preview). Burns do not always mean more moose.
- ICH (Interior cedar and hemlock) clearcuts and burns: moose, mule deer, white tails, elk, cougar; ruffed grouse; black backed and three toed woodpeckers (95%, zone book Table 24).
```

## A burn is a patchwork

```diagram rp-burn-mosaic
```

- Unburned islands, light burn, black burn (85%).
- Hunt the edges between patches (Tip).
- Moose favoured low and medium burn patches (75%).

```more reading severity
- Low severity: ground burned, many trees alive with green crowns, black trunks (Tip).
- High severity: everything black, crowns gone (Tip).
- Unburned islands are bedding and escape cover beside new feed (Tip).
- The Mount McAllister moose study recommends prescribed burns for moose focus on low to medium severity on moist sites (75%).
- Satellite images in mapping apps show burn patches clearly (Tip). See [Map scouting](#/s/map-scouting/0).
```

## Burns bring roads and closures

```photo hab-wells-gray-cutblock Fireguards and salvage logging leave new roads and edges.
```

> [!warn]
> Study aid only. The official regulations are the law.

- Big fires can close areas and even hunting seasons (70%).
- Fireguards open new access into game country (70%).

```more an example from 2017
- In 2017 the Cariboo Fire Centre had 271 fires that burned about 871,036 ha (70%, CBC News, search preview).
- The province closed moose hunting in parts of the Cariboo north of Highway 20 that season to protect moose, and noted that firefighting had created new roads and access into moose habitat (70%, CBC News).
- BC Wildfire Service area restrictions: when one is lifted, officials still have authority under the Wildfire Act to order people out or bring it back (85%, BC Wildfire Service news, search preview).
- Before every trip in fire season, check the BC Wildfire Service for area restrictions and the Rule Book for in season closures (Tip). Region 3 rules: [Region 3](#/s/rb-region-3/0).
```

## Burn hazards

```diagram rp-burn-hazards
```

- Danger trees fall with no warning (85%).
- Ash pits stay hot. Floods and slides follow rain (85%).
- Risks can last 2 years or more (85%).

```more the BC Wildfire Service list
- Danger trees: fire damaged trees that become unstable and can fall without warning (85%).
- Ash pits: hard to detect and can stay hot long after the flames die down (85%).
- Burned ground sheds water: floods and debris flows. In severely burned areas this risk can last much longer than the others (85%).
- Unstable soils, more landslides and rockfall, damaged trails (85%).
- Post wildfire risks may last 2 years or more in severely burned areas (85%).
- Source: BC Wildfire Service news releases and blog, search preview, checked 2026-10-11.
- Bush safety basics: [Bush safety](#/s/bush-safety/0).
```

## Never park or camp under snags

```anim rp-snag-fall A gust, and a burned snag crashes down.
```

- Burned roots let go. Wind brings them down (Tip).
- Park and camp in the open, a tree length from any snag (Tip).

```more snag rules of thumb
- Most snags fall in the first 15 years; few fall in the first 5, then fall fast (65%, snag studies, search preview).
- Windy days and wet soil after rain are the worst times to be among snags (Tip).
- Look up before you sit, glass or field dress (Tip).
- Leaning snags hung up in other trees are the most dangerous (Tip).
```

## Hunt a burn

```steps Hunt a burn in 5 steps
rp-burn-y3 | Pick a burn 3 to 10 years old with shrubs.
rp-zid-1 | Glass it from the far side at first and last light.
rp-dh-3 | Look along unburned islands and edges.
rp-burn-y8 | Walk old roads, not the deadfall.
rp-burn-y15 | Midday: check edges of thick regrowth for beds.
```

- Burns are glassing country: open and wide (Tip).

```more the method in detail
1. Age the burn from the map date or the plants: grass and fireweed (1 to 2 years), knee to chest shrubs (3 to 5), head high pine and aspen (6 to 10) (Tip).
2. Burns are open. Glass from across the valley or from a high corner (Tip). See [Glassing](#/s/glassing/0).
3. Island edges give food and cover side by side (Tip).
4. Deadfall makes noise and twists ankles. Old roads and game trails are quieter (Tip).
5. Fresh beds and droppings at the edge of thickets show where deer and moose lie up (Tip). See [Reading sign](#/s/sign/0).
- Grouse love burn edges with berries and young trees (Tip).
```

## Common mistakes

```diagram rp-burn-hazards
```

> [!mistake]
> - Camping or parking under burned snags.
> - Thinking every burn is full of game. Some burns hold little for years.
> - Walking the deadfall instead of the roads and trails.

```more more mistakes
- Going into a burn under an area restriction (85%, rule above: check first).
- Ignoring a heavy rain forecast on a steep burn. Floods and slides follow rain (85%).
```
