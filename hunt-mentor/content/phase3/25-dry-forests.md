> [!why]
> Most of your October deer hunting near Heffley Creek happens in open pine and Douglas fir. Read this forest well and you know where deer feed, bed and winter.

```photo hab-douglas-fir-stand
```

- Two zones: PP (Ponderosa pine) low, IDF (Interior Douglas fir) above it (95%).
- Open, dry, often park like. You can see far (Tip).
- Mule deer winter range lives here (95%).

```more what this session covers
- PP is the driest and, in summer, the warmest forested zone in BC (95%).
- The IDF has warm dry summers, a fairly long growing season and cool winters; frost can come any month (95%).
- This session: tell pine from fir, why the forest is open, fire then and now, why deer winter under old fir, the deer year here, plants under the trees, who lives here, and how to hunt it.
- Zones overview: [BC habitat zones](#/s/bec-zones/0). Grass below: [Grasslands](#/s/grassland-country/0).
```

## Two zones, one hillside

```diagram rp-bec-ladder
```

- PP: a thin band in the driest valleys, 335 to 900 m (95%).
- IDF: above it, about 350 to 1,450 m (95%).
- Heffley Creek valley is PP. The slopes above are IDF (90%).

```more the climate of each
| | PP (Ponderosa pine) | IDF (Interior Douglas fir) |
|---|---|---|
| Rain and snow a year | 280 to 500 mm (95%) | 300 to 750 mm, over 1,000 mm in the wettest parts (95%) |
| Share as snow | 15 to 40% (95%) | 20 to 50% (95%) |
| Mean July temperature | 17 to 22°C (95%) | warm, dry summers (95%) |
| Main tree | ponderosa pine; Douglas fir in draws (95%) | Douglas fir; lodgepole pine higher (95%) |
| Ground | bluebunch wheatgrass, fescues (95%) | bluebunch wheatgrass on dry sites; pinegrass on moister sites (95%) |

- Heffley Creek reads PPxh2 on the BC zone map; 7 km east the slope reads IDFxh2 (90%, read 2026-10-11).
- Source: Meidinger and Pojar 1991, chapters 9 and 10.
```

## Pine or fir? Read the tree

```diagram rp-pp-vs-df
```

- Long needles in 3s: ponderosa pine (85%).
- Short single soft needles: Douglas fir (85%).
- Needles in 2s, short: lodgepole pine (85%).

```more the full tree key
- Ponderosa pine: tree to 40 to 50 m. Needles mostly in threes, 12 to 20 cm long, yellow green, clustered at branch ends. Old bark cinnamon red in loose plates. Cones 8 to 14 cm with prickly scale tips (85%, E Flora BC).
- Douglas fir: needles 2 to 3 cm, single, soft, spreading around the twig. Bark very thick, rough, dark brown. Interior cones 4 to 7 cm (85%). The 3 pointed bracts that stick out of the cone scales look like mouse tails (85%).
- Lodgepole pine: needles in pairs, 2 to 7 cm, often twisted. Small lopsided cones 3 to 6 cm that can stay shut until fire opens them (85%).
- Western larch, in the southeast and some IDF: needles in bunches of 15 to 30 that turn yellow and fall in autumn (85%).
- Why care: pine means the hottest, driest ground. Thick fir on a north slope means shade and a cool bed (Tip).
```

## Why the forest is open

```anim rp-ground-fire A low fire kills the small trees and leaves the old thick barked pines.
```

- Frequent ground fires kept these forests open (95%).
- Thick bark lets old pines and firs survive (95%).
- Open forest grows grass and shrubs: deer food (95%).

```more fire in the dry forest
- Ponderosa pine is well adapted to fire, and fire has played an important role in the PP zone (95%).
- In the IDF, ground fires were common historically, favouring mature trees with thick bark and open canopies (95%). Where crown fires ran, you get mixed Douglas fir and lodgepole pine with scattered big fir veterans (95%).
- BC rates PP, IDF and BG (Bunchgrass) as NDT4: ecosystems with frequent stand maintaining fires (90%).
- A fire history at Trout Creek Ecological Reserve near Summerland (PPxh1, 75 ha) found a mean fire interval of 18 years from 1715 to 1952 (80%, Gayton 2013, search preview).
- The open canopy lets light through for shrubs like ceanothus and saskatoon that wintering deer and elk eat (95%).
```

## Then and now

```diagram rp-dry-then-now
```

- Less fire for about 100 years means thicker forest (85%).
- Thickets of young fir cut your view and the deer's feed (Tip).
- Burns and thinning open it again (85%).

```more fire suppression and what it changed
- The IDF grasslands have been influenced by reduced fire frequency since the turn of the 20th century (95%, zone book).
- BC restoration guidelines say decades of fire control let dense, even aged stands spread, which later fed beetle outbreaks (85%).
- Restoration work in the dry Interior uses logging, thinning and prescribed burning to reopen fire maintained forests (80%, Kootenay Boundary NDT4 guidelines, search preview).
- Tip for the hunter: a thinned or underburned stand often holds more browse than an unthinned thicket next to it. Look for them on the map and in person.
- Burns in detail: [After fire](#/s/burn-country/0).
```

## Why deer winter under old fir

```diagram rp-dry-winter-range
```

- Big fir crowns catch snow: less snow underneath (95%).
- They drop food: needles, twigs, lichen (95%).
- South slopes add sun (95%).

```more winter range in the zone book
- Low elevation, south facing slopes in the IDF attract many animals in winter (95%).
- In the northern IDF, mule deer require old growth Douglas fir for forage from litterfall and for snow interception (95%).
- In the southern IDF, south aspect forests and dry fir and pine forests are winter habitat for elk, mule deer, white tailed deer and bighorn sheep (95%).
- Denser fir and pine stands give thermal cover for wintering ungulates (95%). See [Food, water and cover](#/s/food-water-cover/0).
- Mule deer key winter foods: big sagebrush, pasture sage, bitterbrush, rabbitbrush, snowbrush, saskatoon, rose, serviceberry and Douglas fir foliage (85%, BC Ministry of Environment).
- Douglas fir needles in the mule deer diet near Kamloops peak in December and January (85%, Willms 1976, from the app's deer research).
- [[Winter range]] is where deer survive or die. Do not push deer on it for sport (Tip).
```

## The deer year in dry forest country

```diagram rp-dry-year
```

- September: many mule deer still high (75%).
- October and November: moving down; rut in November (80%).
- December to March: low south slopes (95%).

```more timing details and sources
- Mule deer often stay at high elevations until December, then migrate to lower ranges with shallower snow (85%, BC Ministry of Environment).
- In summer they leave the dry valley side ranges for moister, higher ground (85%).
- Some travel up to 80 km to winter in the PP and IDF (95%).
- Rut timing for BC deer: [The rut](#/s/the-rut/0). Full migration story: [How animals move](#/s/migration/0).
- Region 3 deer seasons and bag limits: [Region 3 Rule Book](#/s/rb-region-3/0). Study aid only. The official regulations are the law.
```

## Who lives in the dry forest

| Habitat | Animals |
|---|---|
| Open pine parkland | elk, mule deer, white tails, coyote, dusky grouse |
| Pine and fir forest | elk, mule deer, white tails, cougar, black bear |
| Old fir | mule deer, black bear, cougar, dusky grouse |
| Young forest after fire or logging | moose, deer, elk, ruffed grouse |
| Farm fields below | elk, white tails, Canada geese |

- Cougar country is deer country (85%).

```more from the zone book tables
- PP parkland and PP and Douglas fir forests: Rocky Mountain elk, mule deer, white tailed deer, coyote, cougar, black bear, Clark's nutcracker, red crossbill (95%).
- IDF old growth and mature forest: elk, mule deer, white tailed deer, black bear, cougar, bobcat; blue (dusky) grouse (95%).
- IDF young seral forest: moose, elk, mule and white tailed deer, black bear; ruffed grouse (95%).
- Elk and deer in farm areas are often seen as pests because of crop damage (95%).
- Cougar is the top cause of mule deer death in the southern Interior (85%, BC Wildlife Federation, from [Tracks](#/s/tracks/0)).
- Source: Meidinger and Pojar 1991, Tables 20 and 22. Blue grouse is the old name for dusky grouse.
```

## Hunt the open dry forest

```steps Hunt dry forest in 5 steps
rp-dh-1 | First hour: hunt openings and edges.
rp-dh-2 | Still hunt into the wind: 3 steps, then look.
rp-dh-3 | Look for an ear or a leg, not a whole deer.
rp-dh-4 | Midday: check benches and shady draws.
rp-dh-5 | Evening: sit food or water until legal light ends.
```

- Dry needles crunch. Walk the soft pinegrass and game trails (Tip).

```more the method in detail
1. At first light deer are still on feed in openings and edges (Tip).
2. Still hunting: more looking than walking. See [Moving quietly](#/s/still-hunting/0).
3. In open timber, a whole deer is rare. Look for a horizontal back line, an ear, a leg, a flick (Tip). See [Glassing](#/s/glassing/0).
4. Midday beds: benches, the heads of draws, shaded north edges, under big fir (Tip). See [Trails and funnels](#/s/funnels/0).
5. Legal hunting hours: [Legal to shoot](#/s/legal-to-shoot/0).
- Wind in dry forest swirls in draws. Carry a wind checker (Tip). See [Wind and thermals](#/s/wind-thermals/0).
```

## Grandpa's rule

```diagram rp-dry-winter-range
```

> [!rule]
> Grandpa's rule (wisdom, not fact): Old fir is the deer's winter coat. Find the biggest firs on the warmest slope and you have found December.

## Common mistakes

```diagram rp-dry-then-now
```

> [!mistake]
> - Hunting the pine flats at midday. Deer are bedded on benches and in shady draws.
> - Walking fast because you can see far. They see you farther.
> - Ignoring thickets. Young fir thickets hide bedded deer all day.

```more more mistakes
- Calling every pine a ponderosa. Count the needles: 3 long ones or 2 short ones (85%).
- Pushing deer on winter range late in the year for practice (Tip).
```

## Memory hooks

```diagram rp-pp-vs-df
```

- **3 long, 2 short, 1 soft:** ponderosa needles in 3s, lodgepole in 2s, Douglas fir single.
- **Mouse in the cone:** Douglas fir cones have 3 pointed bracts like a mouse hiding with its legs and tail out.
- **18:** old Okanagan pine stands burned about every 18 years.
- **Snow, food, sun:** the 3 jobs of old fir on winter range.
- **Rhyme:** Pine is dry, fir is shy. Open pine to feed, dark fir to hide.
