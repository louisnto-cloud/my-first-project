> [!why]
> A compass never runs out of battery. With five skills (declination, map bearings, triangulation, handrails, aiming off) you can walk to a bench in fog and back to the truck in the dark.

```diagram fn-cmp-error
```

- 1 degree off misses by 17 m per km (98%, maths).
- Forget declination and you miss by 268 m per km.

```more what this lesson adds
- Compass parts, red in the shed and a simple bearing are in [Maps 2: compass, GPS and apps](#/s/maps-navigation/0). Do that lesson first.
- This one adds: setting declination, bearings off the map, finding yourself (triangulation), and the orienteer's tricks: handrails, catching features, attack points, aiming off, pace counting and detours.
- Error maths: miss distance = distance walked times the tangent of the error angle (98%). Per km: 1 degree is 17 m, 5 degrees 87 m, 10 degrees 176 m, 15 degrees 268 m, 30 degrees 577 m.
```

## Key points

| Skill | One line |
|---|---|
| Declination | Set 15° east once a season |
| Map bearing | Ring lines to map north, ignore the needle |
| Orient the map | Turn the map so north lines run with the needle |
| Triangulate | Two bearings, two back lines, one cross |
| Handrail | Follow a line feature |
| Catching feature | Big line just past the target |
| Attack point | Go big, then go small |
| Aiming off | Miss on purpose to one side |
| Pace count | Know your 100 m |

```more the key points in full
1. Near Heffley Creek and Mission the needle points about 15 degrees east of true north (95%, NOAA World Magnetic Model calculator, October 2026).
2. An adjustable compass set to 15 east gives true bearings straight off the ring (90%).
3. Map bearings: line the compass edge from you to the target, turn the ring until its north lines match the map's north lines, read the number. The needle plays no part (95%, orienteering coaching text).
4. Two or more bearings to known landmarks, drawn back on the map, cross at your position. Best when they cross near 90 degrees (85%, resection method).
5. Handrails, catching features, attack points and aiming off are core orienteering techniques (85%, Little Book of Orienteering Techniques; British Orienteering use since the 1970s).
6. Pace counting is a backup for distance, never used alone (75%, same source).
7. Keep steel and electronics away from the needle (Tip).
```

## Declination where you hunt

```diagram fn-cmp-decl
```

- Heffley Creek and Mission: about 15° east (95%).
- South Okanagan: about 14.6° east (95%).

```more declination values and maths
- NOAA (National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration) World Magnetic Model calculator, 15 October 2026 (95%, read directly):
  - Heffley Creek, 50.858 N 120.269 W: 15.17 degrees east, changing minus 0.13 degrees a year.
  - Mission, 49.13 N 122.31 W: 15.20 degrees east, minus 0.13 a year.
  - Osoyoos area, 49.03 N 119.47 W: 14.58 degrees east, minus 0.12 a year.
  - Model uncertainty: about 0.4 degrees (95%).
- These match Maps 2's earlier figure of about 15.1 to 15.2 (80% there, now confirmed at 95%).
- East declination means the needle points right of true north (98%).
- No adjustable compass? Map to compass: subtract 15. Compass to map: add 15 (90%, NRCan rhyme "declination east, magnetic least", see Maps 2).
- Paper NTS (National Topographic System) maps: the blue grid lines are UTM (Universal Transverse Mercator) grid north, not true north. Near Heffley Creek grid north is about 2.5 degrees east of true north, so the needle sits about 12.7 degrees east of the grid lines (85%, my calculation from the UTM zone 10 central meridian at 123 degrees west). The map margin prints this angle for its year. Use it when you take bearings off an NTS grid.
- The Hunt Map and most phone maps draw true north up when the map is not rotated (90%).
```

## Set declination once

```steps Set an adjustable compass to 15° east
fn-decl-set-1 | Find the adjuster: a small screw or a key.
fn-decl-set-2 | Turn it until the orienting arrow sits 15° east.
fn-decl-set-3 | Now red in the shed gives a true bearing.
```

- Do it at home, once a season (Tip).

```more how adjusters differ
- Models differ: some use a tiny screw and a key on the lanyard, some a click ring. Read your compass's own sheet (Tip).
- Set east, not west. Setting 15 west puts you 30 degrees out, 577 m per km (98%, maths; see the mistakes in Maps 2).
- Round to the nearest degree. A compass is good to about 2 degrees in the hand anyway (Tip).
- Check it each spring and fall: declination drifts about 1 degree every 7 or 8 years here (95%, NOAA yearly change).
```

## Take a bearing off the map

```steps Map bearing in 4 steps
fn-mapbrg-1 | Lay the edge from you to the target.
fn-mapbrg-2 | Turn the ring: north lines with map north.
fn-mapbrg-3 | Read the bearing at the index line.
fn-mapbrg-4 | Lift it, red in the shed, walk.
```

- Ignore the needle while on the map (95%).

```more map bearings in full
1. Put the compass on the map with its long edge joining where you are to where you want to go. Travel arrow toward the target (95%, Little Book of Orienteering Techniques).
2. Turn the ring until its north lines run parallel to the map's north lines, N toward the top of the map (95%).
3. Read the number at the index line (95%).
4. Declination set to 15 east? Walk it as it is. Not set? Subtract 15 for an east declination (90%).
5. Hold the compass flat at your belly, turn your whole body until red is in the shed, pick a tree on the line, walk to it, repeat (95%; see Maps 2).
- On paper NTS maps use the grid lines and allow for the grid angle in the map margin (85%).
- On the phone: Tools, Measure in the Hunt Map shows the bearing of the last leg in degrees from true north (99%, app feature). Use it to check your paper work.
```

## Orient the map

```diagram fn-cmp-orient
```

- Turn the map, not yourself, until north lines run with the needle.
- Then the map looks like the land.

```more orienting in full
- "Make sure you are holding the map the same way as the ground features." Turn the map so its north lines run the same way as the needle, red end to north (95%, Little Book of Orienteering Techniques).
- You do not need to turn the compass ring to do it (95%, same source).
- You can also orient by the land: line up a ridge or road on the map with the real one (95%).
- Hold the map and compass in the same hand, thumb on where you are (Tip, orienteering habit "thumb the map").
```

## Find yourself: triangulation

```steps Two bearings, one cross
fn-tri-1 | Take a bearing to a landmark you can name.
fn-tri-2 | Draw its back bearing on the map from the landmark.
fn-tri-3 | Do the same for a second landmark.
fn-tri-4 | Where the lines cross is you.
```

- Pick landmarks about 90° apart (85%).

```more triangulation in full
- Proper name: resection. With three lines people call it triangulation (85%, Wikipedia, Resection).
- Steps: take magnetic bearings to two or more mapped features, correct for declination, convert each to a back bearing (add or subtract 180), draw each line from the feature, and the crossing is your position (85%).
- Accuracy is best when the lines cross near 90 degrees and poor when they are nearly parallel (85%).
- A third line usually makes a small triangle. You are most likely inside it (Tip). Big triangle? Retake the bearings.
- Easier version: on a known handrail (a road, a creek), one bearing to a landmark is enough. Where the back bearing crosses the handrail is you (Tip).
- Or just ask the phone. Then use this skill to check the phone (Tip).
```

## Handrails

```diagram fn-nav-handrail
```

- A line you can follow without the compass: creek, road, fence, ridge, timber edge (85%).
- Worth a detour to use one (85%).

```more handrails in full
- "Easy line features to lead you on your route." They can be tracks, vegetation edges, fences, streams or clearings, or point features joined into a line (85%, Little Book of Orienteering Techniques).
- "It is worth going a bit out of your way to follow them" (85%, same).
- Hunter handrails near Heffley Creek: forest roads, creeks, power lines, cutblock edges (Tip; check each on the map).
- A ridge or a contour line is a handrail too. Following one contour keeps you flat and quiet (Tip).
```

## Catching features

```diagram fn-nav-catch
```

- A big line just past the target.
- Hit it and you know you went too far (85%).

```more catching features in full
- A catching feature is an obvious feature beyond your target that tells you that you have overshot (85%, orienteering sources; Little Book: "features beyond the control to bounce back off").
- Pick one before every leg: a road, a lake shore, the bottom of a valley (Tip).
- For the walk out at dark: the main road is the catching feature for the whole hunt (Tip). See [Night navigation](#/s/night-navigation/0).
```

## Aiming off

```anim fn-nav-aimoff Aim to one side on purpose. Then you know which way to turn.
```

- Aim well left of the truck. Hit the road. Turn right (85%).

```more aiming off in full
- "Aiming deliberately to one side of a feature to know which way to turn when you hit it." (85%, Little Book of Orienteering Techniques)
- Why: a straight bearing at a point on a line is always a little off, and you do not know which way. Aim off and you do (95%, logic).
- How much: enough to beat your error. At 2 km with a 5 degree error you could be 175 m out, so aim off 200 to 300 m (Tip, from the error maths).
- Classic hunter use: the truck on a road, a canoe on a lake shore, a camp on a creek (Tip).
```

## Attack points

```diagram fn-nav-attack
```

- Walk fast to something big and easy, then take a careful short bearing.

```more attack points in full
- An attack point is a large or obvious feature near the target. The safest is where two handrails cross (85%, Little Book of Orienteering Techniques).
- Use it as a preview: go big, then go small (Tip).
- Hunter use: a road bend, a lake corner or a big knob near the bench you want to glass from (Tip).
```

## Pace counting

```diagram fn-nav-pace
```

- Count each left foot on a measured 100 m. Count it again uphill and in brush.
- A backup for distance, never on its own (75%).

```more pace counting in full
- Count double paces, every time one foot lands (75%, Little Book of Orienteering Techniques).
- Know your count for 100 m on a road, flat bush, rough ground and hills. Pace in 100 m blocks and adjust as you go (75%).
- "Never use pacing in isolation, read the map too." (75%)
- Keep track with ranger beads or knots in a cord, one bead per 100 m (Tip).
- Your phone's track distance is another check, if the GPS is good (Tip).
```

## Going around an obstacle

```diagram fn-nav-detour
```

- Box around it: 90° off, count, walk your bearing past it, 90° back, same count.
- Or bearing to a tree across it, walk around, carry on (Tip).

```more detours in full
- Box method: turn 90 degrees, count paces, turn back to the bearing, pass the obstacle, turn 90 degrees the other way, walk the same count, then resume (Tip, standard land navigation habit).
- Sight method: take a bearing to a tree on the far side of the swamp, walk around to the tree, then carry on from it (Tip).
- Do not guess a new line around big obstacles. Small errors add up (Tip).
```

## Errors and metal

```diagram fn-cmp-metal
```

| Error | Fix |
|---|---|
| Steel nearby | Step away from the rifle and the truck |
| Phone in the same hand | Pocket it |
| Compass not flat | Hold it level at your belly |
| Staring at the needle | Pick a tree, walk to it |
| Declination wrong way | Check 15 east at home |

```more errors in full
- Steel, magnets, phones and vehicles bend the needle (80%, common compass guidance). Step a few metres from the truck and lean the rifle away (Tip).
- A tilted compass drags the needle. Hold it level (Tip).
- Sighting at a landmark and walking to it beats walking with your eyes on the needle (95%, see Maps 2).
- Every leg, check against the land: does the ground look like the map? (Tip)
```

## Grandpa's rule and mistakes

```diagram fn-cmp-error
```

> [!rule]
> Grandpa's rule (wisdom): Trust the compass over your gut. Your gut has never been to this draw.

> [!mistake]
> - Aiming straight at the truck on a road. Aim off.
> - Using the needle while taking a bearing off the map.
> - Taking a bearing next to the truck or the rifle.

```more the mistakes in full
- Aiming straight at the truck on a road, then reaching the road and not knowing which way to turn.
- Using the needle while taking a bearing off the map. On the map, only the ring's lines and the map's north lines matter.
- Taking a bearing beside the truck or with the rifle leaning on you. Steel moves the needle.
```
