> [!why]
> Late season goats, sheep and high country deer put hunters on steep snow before the avalanche forecasts even start. A small slope over a gully can bury you.

```diagram fs-av-angle
```

- Most avalanches start on slopes of 30 to 45 degrees (80%).

```more what this session covers
- When avalanches matter to hunters, slope angle, terrain traps, warning signs, the danger scale, terrain ratings, the forecast, rescue gear, travel habits, what to do if caught, and companion rescue.
- This is awareness only. Before travelling in avalanche terrain, take an AST 1 (Avalanche Skills Training 1) course (Tip, Avalanche Canada training).
- Assumption: you will mostly hunt below avalanche terrain in October. Late fall goat, sheep or alpine deer hunts, and any snowmobile access, change that.
```

## Key points

```diagram fs-av-scale
```

- No signs of instability does not mean safe (80%).

```more the 9 key points in full
1. The vast majority of avalanches happen on slopes between 30° and 45° (80%, Avalanche Canada Avy Savvy, read directly).
2. They can happen on 25° to 30° slopes, less often (80%).
3. A slope of about 10 m by 10 m can hold enough snow to be dangerous (80%, Avalanche Canada).
4. Avalanches that start on steep ground can run across flat ground and even uphill (80%).
5. Terrain traps (gullies, creek beds, cliffs, trees, flats under steep slopes) make the result worse (80%).
6. Signs of instability: recent avalanches, whumpfs, shooting cracks (75%).
7. The forecast gives a danger rating from Low to Extreme (80%).
8. Everyone carries a transceiver, probe and shovel, and is trained (75%).
9. Fully buried: over 90% survive the first 15 to 20 minutes, then survival drops fast (75%, Canadian Medical Association Journal commentary).
```

## Is this a hunter problem?

```diagram fs-av-when
```

- Early season snow on grass or rock slabs can slide with little snow (70%).

```more when it matters
- Late season mountain goat and sheep hunts are in steep alpine terrain (Tip). See [Mountain goat](#/s/mountain-goat/0) and [Mountain sheep](#/s/mountain-sheep/0).
- Avalanche Canada has warned that rock slabs, grassy slopes, glaciers and slopes with leftover summer snow need only about 30 cm for avalanches to form (70%, Avalanche Canada blog via search).
- Gullies are natural walking and pack out routes, and they are terrain traps (Tip, 80% for the trap part).
- Snowmobile access: probe and shovel go in the backpack on your back, not on the sled (70%, Avalanche Canada forecaster quoted in news).
- The Kamloops grassland hills near Heffley Creek are mostly gentle. Steep cutbanks and gully walls still count if they are 30° or more and big enough (Tip).
```

## Slope angle

```diagram fs-av-angle
```

```anim fs-av-release A slab cracks, slides and piles deep in the low ground.
```

- Measure it: a phone clinometer app works (80%).

```more slope angle in full
- Avalanche Canada (80%, read directly):
  - "The vast majority of avalanches take place on slopes between 30° and 45°."
  - Slides happen on 25° to 30° slopes but less often.
  - Over 45°, snow usually slides off often, so slabs form less.
  - A slide that starts on steep ground can run across a flat slope and even travel uphill.
  - Use a clinometer or a phone app to measure.
- Avalanche Canada also reported slab avalanches on slopes around 26° with certain buried weak layers (70%, blog via search).
- Convex rolls on a slope are trigger points, and the area right below them is exposed (80%).
- Being below a steep slope counts: you can trigger it from the flat beside or below (75%).
```

## Terrain traps

```diagram fs-av-traps
```

- A small slide into a gully can bury you deep (80%).

```more terrain traps in full
- Avalanche Canada: a terrain trap is any terrain feature that makes the consequences of an avalanche more serious. Examples: gullies, creek beds, ditches, cliffs, flat areas under steep slopes, and trees or rocks in slide paths (80%, read directly).
- Steep sided gullies and creek beds trap moving snow so it piles much deeper in a small area (75%).
- Hunter angle (Tip): we glass from benches and walk creek bottoms. Do not sit or camp in a gully under a loaded slope.
```

## Warning signs

```diagram fs-av-signs
```

- See one? Stay off and out from under steep slopes (Tip).

```more signs in full
- Avalanche Canada glossary, signs of instability (75%, search preview): recent slab avalanches (especially in the last 48 hours), remote triggered avalanches, whumpfs, shooting cracks. Also hollow sounds and snow shedding from trees.
- A whumpf is a buried weak layer collapsing, often with a loud sound (75%).
- Shooting cracks run away from your boots or skis (75%).
- Remote triggering often happens from flatter ground next to avalanche slopes: ridge crests, benches, or right below avalanche paths (75%).
- The absence of signs does not mean there is no danger (75%).
- Heavy new snow, strong wind and rapid warming load or weaken slopes (Tip, VERIFY with Avalanche Canada avalanche problem types).
```

## The danger scale

```diagram fs-av-scale
```

- Considerable (3) is where the most deaths happen (75%).

```more the scale in full
- Avalanche Canada forecasts rate danger as low, moderate, considerable, high or extreme, for each region, for today and the next two days (80%, read directly).
- Low: be cautious, use smart travel habits on steep slopes, avoid shallow rocky start zones (80%).
- Moderate: pay careful attention to the avalanche problems in the forecast (80%).
- Considerable: many slopes should be avoided (80%). The most avalanche deaths happen at this level (75%, search preview).
- High: avoid all avalanche terrain; stick to very mellow slopes or dense trees free of overhead hazard (80%).
- Extreme: avalanches run even into forest (80%).
```

## Terrain ratings

| Class | What it is | You |
|---|---|---|
| Simple | Low angle or mostly forest | Most days OK, watch openings |
| Challenging | Some avalanche paths | Training and a plan |
| Complex | Steep, many paths, traps | Experts only |

```more terrain ratings in full
- The ATES (Avalanche Terrain Exposure Scale) sorts terrain into simple, challenging and complex (80%, Avalanche Canada Avy Savvy outline).
- Simple: low angle or primarily forested; under most conditions you can move confidently, but watch small slopes with traps, forest openings and paths above you (80%, read directly).
- Challenging and complex: definitions not read directly here. The table lines for them are my summary (Tip, VERIFY on Avy Savvy).
- The Avaluator trip planner pairs a terrain rating with the day's danger rating, and is the basis of the AST 1 course (75%).
```

## The forecast

```steps Travel habits in 4 steps
fs-av-travel-1 | Read the forecast the morning you go.
fs-av-travel-2 | Choose ridges, low angle slopes, dense trees.
fs-av-travel-3 | Cross one at a time, safe spot to safe spot.
fs-av-travel-4 | Regroup high and safe, not in gullies.
```

- Before forecasts start: read Avalanche Canada's early season conditions page (70%).

```more forecasts and travel in full
- Check the avalanche forecast before planning your trip (80%, Avalanche Canada).
- Forecast season start dates in past years: 22 to 25 November (70%, search preview). Your October hunts may have no daily forecast. Early season forecasts can say there is not enough data to rate the danger (70%).
- The Mountain Information Network takes observations from anyone (70%).
- Travel habits (Tip, standard avalanche course teaching):
  - One at a time on any slope that could slide, partner watching.
  - Safe spots: ridges, thick trees, high ground away from runouts.
  - Do not stop in runout zones or gullies.
  - Turn back when you see signs of instability.
- Avalanche Canada's daily process: get the forecast, make a trip plan, check gear, verify conditions, good travel habits, watch signs, evaluate slopes, reflect (80%, Avy Savvy outline).
```

## Rescue gear

```diagram fs-av-gear
```

- All three, every person, and practice (75%).

```more gear in full
- Avalanche Canada: the transceiver, probe and shovel are the three key pieces of rescue gear everyone should carry in winter backcountry. One or two of the three is not good enough (75%, search preview).
- Wear the transceiver in its harness and switch it on at the start of the day. Probe and shovel in the pack (75%).
- Snowmobile: probe and shovel in the backpack on your back, not on the sled (70%).
- Training in companion rescue is critical (75%).
- Prices: VERIFY at a Canadian outdoor store. Not checked here.
```

## If you are caught

```steps If caught in 4 steps
fs-av-caught-1 | Yell, then move to the side at 45 degrees.
fs-av-caught-2 | Grab a tree. Shed heavy gear.
fs-av-caught-3 | Swim and fight to stay on top.
fs-av-caught-4 | As it slows: air space, one hand up.
```

- There is no sure way to escape. Avoiding the slope is the real plan (80%).

```more what Avalanche Canada says
- For skiers, read directly (80%), adapted for a hunter on foot (Tip):
  - Yell "AVALANCHE!" to warn others.
  - Move to the side at about 45° down and across the flow.
  - Near the top: try to dig hands into the bed surface.
  - Near a tree: hug it or shelter on its downhill side.
  - In the flow: get rid of skis, poles; for a hunter, the rifle and heavy pack can pull you down (Tip: a pack can also protect you; judgment call).
  - Fight to stay near the surface, swim upward.
  - As it slows: clear snow in front of your mouth with one hand and reach toward the surface with the other.
- Avalanche Canada notes there is no surefire way to escape an avalanche (80%).
```

## Every minute counts

```diagram fs-av-curve
```

- Your partner is the rescue. Help is too far away (Tip).

```more survival in full
- Swiss data: over 90% of fully buried people survive the first 15 to 20 minutes, then survival drops steeply to about 35% by about 35 minutes, mostly from lack of air (75%, Canadian Medical Association Journal commentary, 2011).
- A Canadian study of 301 burials found a faster drop in Canada; the authors suggest about 10 minutes may be a better companion rescue target (75%).
- Fast companion rescue is the main survival tool (75%).
```

## Companion rescue

```steps Companion rescue in 4 steps
fs-av-rescue-1 | Watch and mark the last seen point.
fs-av-rescue-2 | Transceiver to search. Follow the signal.
fs-av-rescue-3 | Probe until you hit. Leave it in.
fs-av-rescue-4 | Shovel hard from downhill. Face first.
```

- Call 911 or SOS once someone is searching, not before you look (Tip, VERIFY in your course).

```more rescue in full
- Avalanche Canada's companion rescue order: signal search, coarse search, fine search, probing, shovelling, when to call for help (80%, Avy Savvy outline, steps not read in detail).
- Watch the person as they go down; the last seen point narrows the search (Tip).
- Probe and shovel details (Tip, standard course teaching): probe perpendicular to the slope in a spiral around the strongest signal; leave the probe in on a strike; dig from the downhill side; clear the airway first.
- Learn this in an AST 1 course with real practice. Reading is not enough (Tip).
```

## Grandpa's rule and mistakes

```diagram fs-av-traps
```

> [!rule]
> Grandpa's rule (wisdom): A goat is not worth a gully full of snow. If the slope talks to you (crack, whumpf), listen and leave.

> [!mistake]
> - "It's only October, no avalanche risk."
> - Glassing from the bottom of a loaded gully.
> - Two hunters, one shovel.

```more the mistakes in full
- Thinking early season means safe. About 30 cm on a smooth slope can slide (70%).
- Sitting, camping or packing meat out in terrain traps (80%).
- Partial gear: one or two of three is not enough (75%).
- Crossing a slope together instead of one at a time (Tip).
- Ignoring whumpfs and cracks because the sun is out (75%).
```
