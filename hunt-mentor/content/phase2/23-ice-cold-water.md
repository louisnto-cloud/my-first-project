> [!why]
> Late season ducks sit on the last open water. Ponds skim over in November. A shortcut across the ice, or a step after a downed bird, is how hunters go through.

## Key points

```diagram fs-ice-colour
```

- Floating ice is never 100% safe (75%).

```more the 8 key points in full
1. Canadian Red Cross thickness for clear blue ice: 15 cm to walk or skate alone, 20 cm for a group, 25 cm for a snowmobile (75%).
2. Clear blue ice is strongest; white opaque ice is about half as strong; grey ice is unsafe (75%).
3. Floating ice on ponds, rivers and lakes is never considered 100% safe (75%).
4. Ice over springs and outlets is thinner; ice on moving water cannot be trusted (75%, Lifesaving Society).
5. Waterfowl keep patches open, so ice near where birds sit can be very thin (70%).
6. Carry ice picks, an ice staff (chisel), a rope and a phone (75%, Lifesaving Society).
7. Fell in: turn back the way you came, arms on the ice, kick, slide out, roll away (75%, Minnesota Department of Natural Resources).
8. Someone else in: call 911, then preach, reach, throw, row. Go only as a last resort (75%).
```

## How thick?

```diagram fs-ice-thickness
```

- Measure several spots. Thickness changes in a few steps (75%).

```more thickness in full
- Canadian Red Cross (75%, search preview): at least 15 cm for walking or skating alone, 20 cm for skating parties or games, 25 cm for snowmobiles.
- These numbers are for clear blue ice (75%, Lifesaving Society).
- Thickness varies with springs, currents, air and water temperature, location, depth and size of the water body (75%).
- Ice sections close together can have very different thickness (75%).
- In October and November the ice is new. Most potholes will not reach 15 cm until well into winter (Tip).
- An [[ATV]] (all terrain vehicle) on ice: no number found in these sources. VERIFY. My lean: never in fall.
```

## Ice colour

```diagram fs-ice-colour
```

- On white ice, double the numbers (70%).

```more colour in full
- Clear blue ice is strongest (75%, Canadian Red Cross).
- White opaque or "snow ice" is about half as strong as clear blue ice (75%, Lifesaving Society). It forms when snow on the ice melts and refreezes (70%, Minnesota Department of Natural Resources).
- Some guidance says double the thickness guidelines on white ice (70%).
- Grey ice often holds water from a thaw and is unsafe (75%, Lifesaving Society).
- White ice with very little air can be nearly as strong as clear ice, so colour is a clue, not a measurement (70%).
```

## Where ice is thin

```diagram fs-ice-weak
```

- Where ducks sit is where ice is thinnest (70%).

```more thin spots in full
- Ice above a spring or an outlet pipe is thinner and weaker (75%, Lifesaving Society).
- Ice on creeks and rivers cannot be trusted; current and changing water levels weaken it (75%).
- Areas used by ducks and geese can have very thin patches because the birds kept the water open longer (70%, municipal advisory).
- Edges near reeds, logs, rocks and docks melt first (Tip).
- Pressure cracks and slush: stay off (Tip).
```

## Test it before you trust it

```steps Test ice in 3 steps
fs-ice-test-1 | Look from shore: open water, grey, slush? Stay off.
fs-ice-test-2 | Chisel a hole and measure. Again every few steps.
fs-ice-test-3 | Spread out, PFD on, picks on your neck.
```

- Any doubt, stay off (Tip).

```more testing in full
- Measuring is the best way to judge ice (75%, Canadian Red Cross: thickness is the best measure).
- An ice chisel (spud) or auger shows thickness; a tape or marked stick measures it (Tip).
- Check more than one spot; ice can be thin in places (75%, Red Cross spokesperson).
- Walk apart, not side by side. One person through is better than two (Tip).
- Wear a PFD (personal flotation device) on the ice; it floats you and pads a fall (Tip; also [Water safety](#/s/water-safety/0)).
```

## The ice kit


- Ice picks around your neck, not in the pack (Tip).

```more kit in full
- Lifesaving Society "Check the ice" material: carry ice picks, an ice staff, a rope and a cell phone (75%).
- Ice picks give the grip you need to pull out; nails or sharpened screwdrivers also work (75%, Minnesota Department of Natural Resources).
- Carry picks for quick access (70%, Colorado Parks and Wildlife).
- Phone in a dry bag or zip bag, on your chest (Tip).
- Dry clothes and a towel in the truck, and the heater ready (Tip).
```

## A bird falls on the ice

```diagram fs-ice-bird
```

- Let it go. No duck is worth falling through (Tip).

```more birds and ice in full
- A common pattern in hunting accidents: a downed bird or a dog on thin ice, and the hunter goes out after it (70%, waterfowl safety coverage).
- Plan shots so birds fall on open water you can reach by boat, or on land (Tip).
- The boat on ice edges: do not push a small boat onto thin ice to retrieve; it can tip as the ice breaks (Tip). See [Water safety](#/s/water-safety/0).
- The law asks for "all reasonable effort to locate, dispatch, retrieve" game you hit (99%, synopsis page 12, see [Rule Book: unlawful acts](#/s/rb-unlawful-acts/0), retrieval screen). My reading: walking onto unsafe ice is not a reasonable effort (Tip, VERIFY with a conservation officer).
- Retrieval by boat has its own rules (loaded guns, motors). Read them on the same Rule Book screen (99%).
```

## You fall through

```steps Get yourself out in 5 steps
fs-ice-self-1 | Expect the gasp. Head up. Breathe 1 minute.
fs-ice-self-2 | Turn back the way you came.
fs-ice-self-3 | Arms on the ice, kick flat, picks in.
fs-ice-self-4 | Slide out, roll away. Do not stand.
fs-ice-self-5 | Get warm and dry right now.
```

```anim fs-ice-selfrescue Breathe, turn back, kick, slide, roll.
```

- Keep your winter clothes on. They trap air (75%).

```more self rescue in full
- Minnesota Department of Natural Resources (75%, read directly):
  - Do not remove your winter clothing; heavy clothes trap air for warmth and flotation.
  - Turn toward the direction you came. That ice is probably the strongest.
  - Place your hands and arms on the unbroken surface. Ice picks, nails or sharpened screwdrivers help grip.
  - Kick your feet and dig in your picks to work back onto solid ice. Lots of water in your clothes? Lift up on your elbows to let it drain first.
  - Lie flat once out and roll away from the hole to spread your weight.
  - Get to a warm, dry, sheltered place and rewarm right away.
- Cold shock gasp, then about 10 minutes of useful movement: use them to get out (80%, the 1 10 1 principle, see [Water safety](#/s/water-safety/0)).
- Can't get out? Get your arms up on the ice and keep still; arms may freeze to the ice and help keep your head up (Tip, VERIFY).
```

## Someone else goes in

```diagram fs-ice-reach
```

- Do not run to the edge of the hole (75%).

```more helping in full
- Minnesota Department of Natural Resources (75%, read directly):
  - First, call 911.
  - Resist the urge to run up to the edge of the hole.
  - Preach: shout to encourage them to fight to survive; help is coming.
  - Reach: from shore, a rope, ladder, branch or pole. Let go if they start pulling you in.
  - Throw: a rope or floating object; have them tie it on before the cold weakens their hands.
  - Row: push a light boat across the ice to the hole, climb in, pull them over the bow.
  - Go: a non professional should not go out on the ice unless every other option is ruled out.
- The partner who rushes out often becomes a second victim (Tip).
```

## After: rewarm carefully

```diagram fs-ice-self-5
```

- Handle gently. Collapse can happen after rescue (75%).

```more after the rescue in full
- Moderate to severe cold water hypothermia needs medical care (75%, Minnesota Department of Natural Resources).
- Cold blood from the limbs can rush back to the heart as you rewarm and cause a dangerous rhythm (75%). See afterdrop in [Cold injuries](#/s/cold-injuries/0).
- After cold water rescue, a person can collapse suddenly ("circumrescue collapse"). Keep them flat and handle gently (75%, Wilderness Medical Society via American Family Physician).
- Wet clothes off once sheltered, dry layers on, insulate under and over, heat to the trunk (75%).
```

## Wading cold creeks

```diagram fs-ice-wade
```

- Over the knee and fast: find another crossing (Tip).

```more wading in full
- All Tip (outdoor and hunter practice, not read in an official source here):
  - Scout for a wide, shallow, slow spot. Not above a logjam or a drop.
  - Face upstream at an angle; shuffle; one foot moves at a time.
  - A stout pole or trekking pole as a third leg, on the upstream side.
  - Undo the pack hip belt and sternum strap so you can shed it if you fall.
  - Rifle unloaded and slung, action open or cased (see [Firearm safety](#/s/firearm-safety/0)).
  - Cross early in the day; glacier and snow fed creeks rise in the afternoon.
- Wet feet in November: change socks right away (see [Cold injuries](#/s/cold-injuries/0)).
- Waders can fill and pull you down if you fall. Wear a wading belt cinched tight (Tip, see [What to wear](#/s/what-to-wear/0)).
```

## Grandpa's rule and mistakes

```diagram fs-ice-bird
```

> [!rule]
> Grandpa's rule (wisdom): Fall ice is a liar. If you have to ask whether it will hold you, it won't.

> [!mistake]
> - Walking out after a duck on skim ice.
> - Two hunters side by side on the ice.
> - Taking off the coat in the water.

```more the mistakes in full
- Going out on thin ice after a bird (70%).
- Walking together instead of apart (Tip).
- Shedding winter clothing in the water; it traps air (75%).
- Running to the hole to help, and going in too (75%).
- Trusting white or grey ice like clear ice (75%).
```
