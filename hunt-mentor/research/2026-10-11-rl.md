## Terrain features (lesson 13)
- land-navigation.com, Terrain features, https://www.land-navigation.com/terrain-features.html (read, 75%): hill; saddle "rises on two sides and falls on two sides", also pass or col; valley and draw contours "point toward higher ground"; ridge and spur contours point "toward lower ground"; spur often extends out from a ridge; cliff contours "converging, or nearly converging"; depression tick marks; cut and fill.

## Slope, aspect, winter range (lessons 14, 15, 18)
- Wildlife habitat rating account, Mule deer, BC Ecological Reports Catalogue (Chilcotin and Fraser Plateau project area), 2004, https://a100.gov.bc.ca/pub/acat/documents/r1573/whr_4109_modhe_1097078581992_3f7c787cc7c9486aa185bdad7e4c4f53.pdf (read, 85%):
  - Spring green up feeding "on moderate to steep, south and west facing slopes".
  - "If snow accumulations exceed 50 cm then an area is generally avoided" (Ehlers et al. 1998).
  - Deer spend most energy in "crustless, dense, deep snow (i.e. sinking depths greater than 25 cm)" (Nyberg and Janz 1990).
  - Night thermal cover: closed canopy above a deer's head or above 3 m, less wind, intercepts snow. Day: "areas that gather heat (on or near rock bluffs) or intercept excessive solar radiation" (Bunnell et al. 1985).
  - Snow interception: "stands taller than 10 m with greater than 60% crown completeness".
  - North aspects "support denser stands of higher crown closures" with good snow interception; used in severe weather.
  - Security cover "hides 90% of the animal at a distance of 60 m or less, and security patches need to be 180 m or more in diameter"; "Areas of steep broken terrain can also be used as security habitat" (Nyberg and Janz 1990).
  - Winter range "10 to 45 % slope, having a south and/or west aspect, and below 1500 m in shallow to moderate snowpack zones or below 1000 m in deep snowpack zones" (US Forest Service 1998, as cited).
  - Spring: "security/thermal cover near forage habitat (i.e. within 200 m)".
  - Douglas fir 62 to 89% of winter diet at Knife Creek near Williams Lake (Waterhouse et al. 1994).
- BC Forest Investment Account, Silviculture best management practices, Key Habitat Objectives 7.6.1, Mule deer winter range, February 2004, https://www.env.gov.bc.ca/fia/documents/mca_silvbmp/mca_silvbmp_2.pdf (read, 85%): critical winter habitat is mature and old Douglas fir "with closed, multi-layered canopies (generally > 60% canopy closure), on moderate to steep slopes (approximately > 40%), on warm, southerly aspects in low and moderate snowpack areas"; warm aspects defined as 135 to 270 degrees; cool aspects 315 to 90 degrees; snowpack zones shallow under 100 cm per year, moderate 100 to 150, deep 150 to 200.
- Thomas et al. 1979 elk cover, as cited (search preview, 70%): thermal cover a conifer stand at least 40 ft (12 m) tall with over 70% crown closure; hiding cover hides 90% of a standing elk at 61 m (200 ft). Cook et al. 1998 (Blue Mountains) found thermal cover did not improve elk condition (US Forest Service white paper, https://www.fs.usda.gov/media/168576, search preview, 70%).
- Germaine, Germaine and Boe 2004, Wildlife Society Bulletin 32(2): 554 to 564, mule deer day beds in Arizona ponderosa pine, https://www.esf.edu/biology/faculty/documents/Germaineetal2004muledeerdaybedsites.pdf (abstract read, 80%): 236 summer day beds; "Site temperature and canopy closure were the most influential attributes".
- Mule deer summer beds in Montana and Idaho sage steppe preferred cooler northeast and east aspects and overhead cover (Montana State thesis, search preview, 60%).
- Armstrong et al. 1983, white tailed deer winter day beds in central Ontario in sun exposed spots (search preview, 60%).
- Avalanche Canada, slope angle, https://avalanche.ca/glossary/terms/slope-angle and https://avysavvy.avalanche.ca/en-ca/slope-angle (search preview, 85%): most avalanches start on 30 to 45 degree slopes; they can release from about 25 to 60 degrees.
- Sun height at noon: 90 minus latitude plus solar declination (astronomy, 98%). Kamloops area latitude about 50.7 N (the app's Heffley base is 50.858 N, app/map/core.js). Mid October declination about minus 9 degrees, so noon sun about 30 degrees up; 21 December about 16 degrees. Worked out here (95%).
- Slope maths: percent = tan(degrees) x 100 (98%). 10 deg 18%, 15 deg 27%, 20 deg 36%, 25 deg 47%, 30 deg 58%, 35 deg 70%, 40 deg 84%, 45 deg 100%.

## Escape terrain (lesson 19)
- Management Plan for the Mountain Goat in BC, 2010, https://www.env.gov.bc.ca/wld/documents/recovery/management_plans/MtGoat_MP_Final_28May2010.pdf (read, 90%): escape terrain "generally steep slopes usually >= 40 degrees or >= 84% of shear or broken cliffs"; goats "usually reluctant to venture more than 400 to 500 m from escape terrain, often less distance during winter"; river valley goats use steep mud and clay banks.
- Bighorn Sheep in BC, BC Ministry of Environment, 2000, https://www.env.gov.bc.ca/wld/documents/bighorn.pdf (read, 85%): "best suited ... to rugged, mostly treeless terrain"; winter ranges "steep, south to southwest facing", bluebunch wheatgrass, "rugged breaks and terraces along river valleys, rocky outcrops"; ewes lamb in "rugged lambing cliffs"; young sheep learn "escape terrain" from older sheep.
- Texas bighorn study: ewes within about 250 yd and rams about 300 yd of escape terrain; preferred 40 to 79% slopes (Borderlands Research Institute, search preview, 60%, desert sheep).
- Lingle 2002, Ecology, coyote predation and habitat segregation of white tailed and mule deer (abstract via search preview, 75%): mule deer moved "to and up slopes" when coyotes came; white tails moved "down and away"; mule deer bunch and stand, white tails flee. Dellinger 2019: stotting works best on uneven ground (search preview, 70%).
- California quail "seldom ventures more than 15 to 30 m from cover", CDFW life history (already logged, 75%).

## Edges (lesson 17)
- Smith, Managing Edge Habitat, Alabama Cooperative Extension System, 19 September 2022, https://www.aces.edu/blog/topics/forestry-wildlife/managing-edge-habitat/ (read, 75%): "Edge is defined as the juncture of two or more different cover types"; ecotone or transitional edge; hard edge; too much edge hurts some forest birds; edges attract nest predators.

## Roads, hunters and islands (lessons 21, 22)
- Luloff, Finley, Diefenbach, Stedman and others, A Comparison of Hunter Movement, Activities and Opinions During Two Pennsylvania Hunting Seasons, Final Report, August 2004, https://ecosystems.psu.edu/research/pacfwru/reports/hunting-seasons/@@download/file/Final-huntmvmt-082004.pdf (read, 85% for that forest; 60% when applied to BC):
  - "hunter density was negatively related to distance from roads and slope".
  - Hunters "nearly 3 times more likely to hunt in an area for each additional km closer to the nearest road" (odds ratio 2.97) and 1.25 times more likely for every 10 degree drop in slope.
  - "average maximum distance hunters reached from a road open to public vehicles was 0.84 km"; they walked 5.48 km a day.
  - Hunters reported walking more than 2.5 times farther than their GPS showed (2.23 km self reported, JWM 2004 abstract via search preview).
  - 16% of the study area was over 1 km from a road open to the public.
- Hillis et al. 1991 elk security areas: 250 acres (about 100 ha) at least half a mile (0.8 km) from open roads; never validated, debated (Backcountry Hunters and Anglers letter and Ranglack et al. 2017, search preview, 60%).
- Rule Book (this app, content/phase7, 2 October 2026 edition): road allowance 15 m; 400 m single projectile routes (Hwy 5 Hope to Kamloops listed, north of Kamloops not, my reading in the Rule Book); 100 m rule; park roads 400 m; item 22 no hunting from a motor vehicle; item 28 no loaded firearm in or on a vehicle; item 38 no hunting with a light; definition of hunt includes "searching for" wildlife while in possession of a firearm.
