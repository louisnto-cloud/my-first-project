## Primary sources read directly (full text)

1. BC Ministry of Environment, Lands and Parks (2000), *Elk in British Columbia: Ecology, Conservation and Management* (text D. A. Blood). https://www.env.gov.bc.ca/wld/documents/elk.pdf . Read directly. Use 90% for biology lines.
   - Two subspecies in BC: Roosevelt (C. e. roosevelti) and Rocky Mountain (C. e. nelsoni). Look similar; Roosevelt slightly larger and darker; Roosevelt antlers sometimes end in a crown of 3 or 4 points.
   - Brownish coat, dark mane, white (yellowish white) rump patch bordered by dark brown or black stripe; large rounded upper canines (elk tusks) in both sexes. Only North American deer other than caribou that commonly form large social groups.
   - Mature bulls (4 years and older): antlers 110 to 160 cm long beams, usually five other tines plus the tip. Yearlings usually spikes; 2 year olds 3 or 4 points a side (raghorns); 3 year olds 4 or 5.
   - Winter coat: head, neck, legs dark brown; sides and back lighter grey brown. Bulls lighter, creamier body. Summer coat sleek, rich reddish brown, little or no undercoat. Spring moult May and June, ragged.
   - Adult bulls about 140 cm at shoulder, 265 to 410 kg; cows about 130 cm, 190 to 270 kg.
   - Roosevelt elk in BC: 3,000 to 3,500 on Vancouver Island (2000), small herd Phillips Arm, introduced herds near Sechelt and Powell River. Extirpated on Gulf Islands and Lower Mainland by settlement.
   - Rocky Mountain elk: most numerous in the east and west Kootenay, north to about Golden, west to Grand Forks; east slope of the Rockies to the Liard; Muskwa and Tuchodi.
   - Transplants from 1917 (first: 10 animals near Lillooet); over 600 released; new herds incl. Princeton, Lytton, Okanagan Lake, Granby River, Lower Arrow Lake, Sechelt, Powell River, Queen Charlotte Islands.
   - Interior Rocky Mountain elk rose from about 15,000 (mid 1970s) to 40,000: 18,000 north, 20,000 Kootenay, 1,350 Thompson Okanagan.
   - Cow groups: up to 20 or more cows, calves and yearlings, apart from smaller bull groups except in the rut. Dominance by threats and displays; front hooves as weapons; sparring not true fights.
   - Mid September rut; harems of several cows; bugle, wallow, spray urine, thrash vegetation. Bugle: low chesty roar, glides into a high bugling sound, ends in grunts. Serious fights, occasional goring.
   - Most cows bred mid to late September; others about a month later; possibly a third in November. Gestation eight months. Calves late May and early June. One spotted calf about 13 kg; twins rare. Cow hides calf in dense cover, returns to nurse; after two or three weeks they rejoin the matriarchal group.
   - Most cows first breed just over two years old. Bulls can breed as yearlings but usually do not until 4 or 5. Can live to 20; most die by 10 or 12.
   - Antlers drop late February to early April, oldest first. Grow from April, full size by late August or early September; velvet rubbed off.
   - Bulls do not feed in the rut and start winter in poor condition; higher death rates in bulls; sex ratio favours cows. Malnutrition in severe winters probably the main reason adults die. Main predators: wolves, cougars, bears, mostly calves and weakened adults.
   - Elk usually keep to regions with shallow snow on winter range; less adapted to snow than moose.
   - Roosevelt herds: small scattered herds in river valleys; sedges, grasses, ferns, willow, elderberry, blueberry, cedar, hemlock browse; most go up to subalpine meadows and avalanche tracks in summer, a few stay low.
   - Rocky Mountain elk more migratory. Winter ranges: open forest, grassy benchlands, floodplain marshes. Winter foods: sedges, horsetail, willow; bunchgrasses, forbs, serviceberry (saskatoon), rose, other shrubs. Relish alfalfa and clover. May and June: migrate to subalpine and alpine basins and avalanche tracks; bulls first, cows wait for calves. Autumn snow pushes them back to traditional winter ranges; a few bulls stay high.
   - "Hunted Elk become wary, secretive, and nocturnal and learn the location of protective reserves."
   - Elk are intelligent animals that learn from experience. Harvests 500 to 4,000 a year over decades. Roosevelt elk added to Blue List in 1998.

3. BC Species Habitat Model, Rocky Mountain Elk, La Biche and Sandy Creek (Fort Nelson area), a100.gov.bc.ca, https://a100.gov.bc.ca/pub/acat/documents/r1651/whr_4012_mceel_1098209135230_07a33d337dd7428586868202f6bc05bd.pdf . Read directly. 80% (model text citing studies).
   - Roosevelt elk blue listed, Vancouver Island and Sunshine Coast; Rocky Mountain elk patchy in SE and NE BC with smaller populations in the Okanagan and near Lytton. Greatest densities East Kootenay and Muskwa Kechika.
   - Winter: paw through snow; crust or 30 cm or more: move to shrub and conifer habitats; over 60 cm reduces mobility, shift to browse (RIC 1997). San Juan Mountains: near 40 cm move to less snow; over 70 cm severely limits movement (Sweeney and Sweeney 1984).
   - Security cover hides 90% of a standing elk at 61 m or less (Thomas 1979). In disturbed area elk forage within 100 to 200 m of cover by day, farther into the open at night (Morgantini 1979). Summer: bed wherever done feeding but close to cover.
   - Rut second to third week of September in Muskwa Tuchodi (personal communication).
4. BC Species Habitat Model, Rocky Mountain Elk, Muskwa area, a100.gov.bc.ca, https://a100.gov.bc.ca/pub/acat/documents/r1583/whr_4069_mceel_1117055960977_ad101d33f76a47c38b67f114e5fbb078.pdf . Read directly. 80%.
   - Habitat and diet generalists; edges between forest and non forest; migration driven by vegetation and snow depth. Grazers in the growing season; grasses and sedges used year round; browse in winter (saskatoon, water birch, aspen).
   - Snow depth most limiting; movement restricted past 46 cm (Beall 1974); browse replaces grazing past 61 cm (Skovlin 1982).
   - North slopes coolest in summer; elk prefer south to southwest exposures in winter and spring.
   - Escape cover: hides 90% of an elk from a human at 61 m. Winter thermal cover: closed conifer over 12 m, canopy over 70%.
   - Most elk within 183 m of cover (Black 1979). Slope use rises to 30 to 40%; 15 to 30% most used; use drops sharply past 40 to 50%.
   - Rut September through October; calves late May or June.
   - Elk have a larger rumen to body size ratio than mule deer and bighorn sheep, may compete better on poor range.
5. Wisdom and others (2004), *Effects of off road recreation on mule deer and elk*, Transactions of the 69th North American Wildlife and Natural Resources Conference, US Forest Service PNW. https://www.fs.usda.gov/pnw/pubs/journals/pnw_2004_wisdom001.pdf . Read directly. 85%.
   - Elk movement rates highest during ATV riding, then mountain bike, then hiking and horseback. Movement also higher around sunrise and sunset on treatment days.
   - Flight probability: little effect when hikers were beyond 500 m; higher probabilities beyond 750 m from horse riders and beyond 1,500 m from mountain bike and ATV riders. Maximum about 0.65 (ATV, bike, hiking) and 0.55 (horse) when close. Mule deer showed less change.
6. Wisdom and others (2018), *Elk responses to trail based recreation on public forests*, Forest Ecology and Management 411: 223 to 233. https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/56220 . Read directly (abstract and introduction). 85%.
   - Elk avoided trails during recreation, shifted out of view; mean minimum separation from people 558 to 879 m; highest for ATV, lowest for hiking and horseback.
7. University of Wyoming Extension Bulletin B-594R, *The elk carcass*. https://www.wyoextension.org/agpubs/pubs/B-594R.pdf . Read directly. 85%.
   - Bull weighed through processing: whole 568 lb (258 kg); field dressed 400 lb (181 kg) = 0.70 of whole; skinned carcass 332 lb (151 kg) = 0.83 of field dressed; boneless lean 166 lb (75 kg) = 0.50 of field dressed.
   - Six bulls averaged 437 lb (198 kg) field dressed and 218 lb (99 kg) boneless; six cows 339 lb (154 kg) and 169 lb (77 kg). One locker plant averaged 43%. Rutting reduces fat in bulls.
