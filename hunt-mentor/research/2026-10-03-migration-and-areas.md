## 1. Seasonal movement by species

### Mule deer (southern interior)
- BC account, Mule and Black tailed Deer in BC (Ministry of Environment, Lands and Parks, 2000), https://www.env.gov.bc.ca/wld/documents/muledeer.pdf, checked 2026-10-03:
  - Deer "have difficulty moving through snow deeper than 30 cm", so they cannot winter in the alpine and subalpine zones (85%).
  - "In summer, most deer migrate to higher elevations ... but some remain at low elevations all year." (85%)
  - Interior winter range: "shrublands in the dry forest zone and on steep south and west facing sites with broken terrain" (85%).
  - "They often remain at high elevations until December, then migrate to lower ranges with shallower snow. In summer, they usually leave the dry, valleyside ranges and move to moister, higher elevations." (85%)
  - Best viewing is April on "low elevation, south facing grassy slopes", named sites include "the Dewdrop Range near Kamloops" (85%).
  - Rut "in November and early December"; fawns "late May through June" (85%).
- SIMDeer (Southern Interior Mule Deer Project), BCWF:
  - Project page https://bcwf.bc.ca/initiatives/mule-deer-project/ : 30,000 km2 study; "74 per cent were migratory, travelling an average of 47.2 km per year"; main cause of death predation by cougars, bears and coyotes (85%).
  - Winter update, 11 March 2020, https://bcwf.bc.ca/southern-interior-mule-deer-project-winter-update/ : "78% of our deer migrated. Most deer moved to summer range April-May and returned to winter range in October-Nov. On average the collared deer migrated 55 kms ... longest migrant ... approximately 100 kms. Most of the deer choose nearly the same path" (85%).
  - Update, 13 November 2019, https://bcwf.bc.ca/southern-interior-mule-deer-project-update/ : a doe collared near Apex Mountain west of Penticton left on 1 May and arrived west of Aspen Grove on 12 May, 102 km in a straight line; "By mid-October this year, nearly all the collared deer in each study area were back in their winter range." (85%)
  - Study areas: West Okanagan, Cache Creek, Boundary (85%). Cache Creek is the closest to Heffley Creek (85%).
- Trigger: snow and cold, not the rut. Monteith 2011, reused from deer notes (85%).
- Snow: 30 cm "difficulty" (BC account, 85%) versus "avoid more than about 50 cm" (UBC thesis, deer notes, 80%). Present as a range: trouble starts near 30 cm, deer leave near 50 cm (my reading, 75%).
- Kamloops winter range: Dewdrop range below 760 m north of Kamloops, and Jamieson Creek (Willms 1976, deer notes, 80%).
- Timing conflict: SIMDeer October to November (and mid October in 2019) versus the 2000 account "often remain high until December". Use October to December as the window, earlier in deep snow years (my reading, 75%).
- Elevations: no collar elevation data found. Range built from BEC (biogeoclimatic ecosystem classification) zones in section 2: winter about 300 to 1,000 m (BG, PP, IDF), summer about 900 to 2,000 m (MS, ESSF) (65%, general pattern).

### White tailed deer
- BC account, White tailed Deer in BC (Blood 2000), https://www.env.gov.bc.ca/wld/documents/whttail.pdf, checked 2026-10-03 (85% as read; the account is built mostly on Kootenay data, so 70% when applied to the Thompson and Okanagan):
  - "Climate, particularly the depth of the snow, is the main factor in determining the distribution" (85%).
  - "Within areas that have a suitable climate, whitetails prefer valley bottoms with farmland." (85%)
  - Winter (East Kootenay): south to southwest slopes, fans and terraces with shrubs; aspen, cottonwood and willow groves along rivers and wetlands "in early winter before the snow gets too deep"; conifer patches where snow is deep (85%).
  - "In summer, the whitetails migrate to floodplains and adjacent terraces." (85%)
  - "Seasonal movements are mostly upstream or downstream along the major valleys, or cross-valley from north-facing slopes in summer to south-facing sites in winter." (85%, quoted with the source's hyphens)
  - Rut peaks in November; most fawns born late May or June (85%).
  - "In summer, they are more active at dawn and dusk, but in winter they are active most of the day." (85%)
- Pattern for the app: resident with short seasonal moves, valley bottom all year (75%).

### Black tailed deer (coast, Fraser Valley, Hope)
- BC account 2000 (same PDF as mule deer): old growth is key winter range for coastal black tails (shelter, snow interception, litterfall forage); "northward and at higher elevations ... steep, south to west facing sites provide the best winter range" (85%).
- Gold River Wildlife Interpretations, black tailed deer (BC Ecological Reports Catalogue, Madrone Consultants, citing RIC 1998 and Nyberg and Janz 1990), https://a100.gov.bc.ca/pub/acat/documents/r1773/whr_4064_modhe_1116621873479_669615d733154125b483be52fe74517e.pdf, checked 2026-10-03:
  - "elevations greater than 1000 m are rarely used as winter habitat" (80%).
  - "During the winter, deer are restricted to lower elevations due to snowpack." Winter: low, south facing warm slopes or floodplains (80%).
  - "Black-tailed deer breed during November and early December." (80%)
  - Resident or migratory. Migratory annual home range 1,770 ha versus 140 ha for residents in a moderate snowpack zone (Nyberg and Janz 1990) (80%). Migrants move within a watershed (80%).
- McNay and Voller, Vancouver Island, 95 collared does 1982 to 1991, https://www.for.gov.bc.ca/hfd/pubs/Docs/Bro/Bro41.pdf : annual survival 74%; residents 77%, migrants 90%; old forests at low elevation essential (80%).
- Spring departure timing: "most migratory deer left winter ranges during March" appears only in a search preview (55%, VERIFY).
- No Fraser Valley or Hope collar study found. Weak species: dates are Vancouver Island based (60 to 70% for area B).

### Moose
- BC account, Moose in BC (2000), https://www.env.gov.bc.ca/wld/documents/moose.pdf, checked 2026-10-03 (85%):
  - Seasonal home range "seldom exceeds 5 to 10 km2"; annual range larger for migratory moose.
  - Mate September to November; more than 80% of calves conceived in the first heat, "two weeks in late September and early October".
  - Calves born late May and June.
  - "little or no difficulty moving around in snow up to 40 cm deep and only slight difficulty in snow depths of 40 to 70 cm deep"; "commonly winter in areas with 50 cm or more of snow cover".
  - Mountains: "usually migrate between winter-spring ranges in valley bottoms and higher-elevation summer ranges, but some Moose remain in the valleys year-round." In autumn they "stay on high-elevation ranges much longer than Elk, deer, or mountain sheep", return gradually; light snow keeps them at mid elevation until late winter.
  - Plateaus: summer spread out near "lake shores, swamps, and Beaver ponds"; winter "concentrate along river valleys that cut through the plateaus and in burns, logged areas, and wetland complexes".
  - Winter browse: rivers, wetlands, burns, logged areas, lower avalanche tracks; red osier dogwood, cottonwood, birch, aspen, willow, subalpine fir.
- Bonaparte Plateau, north of Kamloops, is a provincial moose research study area since February 2012 (2016 progress report, https://www2.gov.bc.ca/assets/gov/environment/plants-animals-and-ecosystems/wildlife-wildlife-habitat/moose/2016_moose_research_progress_report.pdf) (90%). Its population was stable at study start (85%). No movement results found in that report.

### Elk
- BC account, Elk in BC (2000), https://www.env.gov.bc.ca/wld/documents/elk.pdf, checked 2026-10-03 (85%):
  - About 1,350 elk in the Thompson Okanagan area in 2000 (85%). Few elk near our bases (my reading).
  - Rut "mid-September"; "Most cows are bred from mid to late September"; calves late May and early June.
  - Interior Rocky Mountain elk "tend to be more migratory"; winter on open forest, grassy benchlands, floodplain marshes; "In May and June, most Rocky Mountain Elk migrate to subalpine and alpine basins and avalanche tracks"; bulls go first; "Autumn snowfalls push the Elk back to their traditional winter ranges".
  - Coastal Roosevelt elk: herds tied to major river valleys; "In summer, most Roosevelt herds migrate upward to subalpine meadows and avalanche tracks, but a few stay year-round on valley-bottom ranges."
  - Elk "usually keep to regions where the snow remains shallow on winter ranges".
- Lower Mainland: Roosevelt elk released near Chehalis under the Coastal Mainland Roosevelt Elk Recovery and Management Project (HCTF (Habitat Conservation Trust Foundation), 30 November 2015, https://hctf.ca/return-of-the-roosevelts/) (85%). Stave watershed (2006 to 2007) and Pitt River watershed (2004 to 2005) releases: news search preview, Times Colonist (65%).

### Bighorn sheep
- BC account, Bighorn Sheep in BC (2000), https://www.env.gov.bc.ca/wld/documents/bighorn.pdf, checked 2026-10-03 (85%):
  - California bighorns: Okanagan (Ashnola, Vaseux Lake to Penticton Creek, Shorts Creek), Fraser River Lillooet to Williams Lake; introduced at Kamloops Lake, Grand Forks, Dog Creek.
  - Breed "between early November and mid-December"; lambs "from the last week in April to early June" in rugged lambing cliffs near the winter and spring range.
  - Winter and spring: "low-elevation grasslands in the Bunchgrass, Ponderosa Pine, and Interior Douglas-fir zones ... at elevations of from 300 to 1825 m", steep, south to southwest, bluebunch wheatgrass.
  - "Most migratory bighorns leave their winter ranges in May or June and move to summer pastures in the Alpine Zone"; "return to their winter ranges in late September or October when snow covers their alpine forage".
  - Many Fraser River bighorns now stay all year on low ranges (nonmigratory).
- Dewdrop Rosseau Creek WMA (Wildlife Management Area): winter range for mule deer and California bighorn sheep (BC WMA page, 90%). McTaggart Cowan nsək'łniw't WMA: lambing, escape terrain, winter and spring range for California bighorn; winter habitat for mule deer, elk, mountain goat (BC WMA page, 90%).

### Mountain goat
- Management Plan for the Mountain Goat in BC (2010), https://www.env.gov.bc.ca/wld/documents/recovery/management_plans/MtGoat_MP_Final_28May2010.pdf, checked 2026-10-03 (85%):
  - "Coastal ecotype mountain goats typically winter at moderate to lower elevations in forested habitats", interior ecotype at higher elevations with less snow.
  - "Interior populations ... using higher elevation at or above treeline during summer and fall, and lower elevations including forests during spring and early summer", for green up and mineral licks.
  - Interior winter: high windswept slopes, or rocky bluffs at and below treeline where snow is heavier.
  - Rarely more than 400 to 500 m from escape terrain (slopes 40 degrees or more).
  - Breed "from early November to early December, normally peaking 15–20 November".
  - Lick use April to early autumn; trips up to 24 km to licks.
  - Critical periods: winter 1 November to 30 April; kidding and early rearing 1 May to 15 July.
- Approved goat winter ranges exist in area B (orders u-2-001, u-2-002) and area C (u-3-006, u-8-005, u-8-009) (UWR (ungulate winter range) layer query, 90%).

### Black bear (denning)
- BC account, Black Bears in BC (2001), https://www.env.gov.bc.ca/wld/documents/blackbear.pdf, checked 2026-10-03 (85%):
  - Hibernate "three to five months on the south coast and for longer periods (probably five to seven months) in the interior and the north"; pregnant females den longer.
  - Cubs born January or February.
  - Coast dens in or under big trees, snags, logs or stumps; interior dens also in rock cavities, brush piles or dug holes.
  - Spring food: meadows, estuaries, riparian, skunk cabbage, avalanche chutes, grassy south slopes, burns. Autumn: berries and spawning fish.
  - Home ranges: males 25 to 150 km2, females 5 to 25 km2.
- Gaines 2003, Canadian Field Naturalist 117(4), northeastern Cascades of Washington (next to the Similkameen and Okanagan), https://www.canadianfieldnaturalist.ca/index.php/cfn/article/download/833/833/3326 : males entered dens 22 October to 19 November, out 4 April to 7 May; females in 15 October to 19 November, out 4 April to 22 May; dates similar to northwestern Montana and southwestern BC (Allen 2001) (80% for our areas).
- WildSafeBC, https://wildsafebc.com/species/black-bear/ : "typically in dens from December to April"; elsewhere "typically ranging from November until April"; emerge in April; some bears, often males, do not den if food is available (75%).
- Window for the app: interior den entry mid October to late November, out April to May; coast later in and earlier out (75%).

### Ducks and geese (Pacific Flyway)
- Ducks Unlimited field reports (DU Canada data), checked 2026-10-03 (75%):
  - 2024 update, https://www.ducks.org/conservation/waterfowl-research-science/field-reports-2024-migration-and-wintering-habitat-update : in BC "The fall migration largely concluded by late November, coinciding with freeze-up in most of the province."
  - Outlook page, https://www.ducks.org/conservation/national/field-reports-migration-and-wintering-habitat-outlook : interior warm fall "delaying freeze-up until late November"; south coast "Large flocks of snow and cackling geese" in fields.
  - 2025 fall outlook, https://www.ducks.org/conservation/waterfowl-research-science/field-reports-2025-fall-habitat-outlook : "The fall migration was well under way in late September, with the first snow geese arriving along the coast."
- Fraser River delta (outside the Mission box, but the flyway hub):
  - PBHJV (Pacific Birds Habitat Joint Venture) 2024 guide, https://pacificbirds.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/Conservation-of-Mig-Bird-Habitat-Fraser-River-Delta-Guide-for-Local-Govts-2024.pdf : waterfowl food demand runs "from September to April"; about 240,000 waterfowl supported (text extraction messy, 75%); a refuelling site on the Pacific Flyway (85%).
  - Boundary Bay WMA page: "a vital link in the Pacific Flyway, supporting over 1.5 million birds" (90%).
- Snow geese, Reifel Migratory Bird Sanctuary (BC Waterfowl Society), https://www.reifelbirdsanctuary.com/snow.html (80%): Wrangel Island (Russia) birds; arrive "by early October"; best viewing "mid-Oct. to mid-Dec. and mid-March to mid-April"; late December to February nearly all move to the Skagit estuary (Washington), return in spring, leave "in April for Wrangel Island"; January 2021 mid winter count of Fraser and Skagit flocks 135,000; 5,000 km trip.
- Fraser Valley WMAs, BC WMA pages (90%):
  - Pitt Addington Marsh WMA, south end of Pitt Lake: "wintering, migration and breeding habitat for waterfowl"; Greater Sandhill Cranes.
  - Bert Brink WMA (915 ha, Fraser floodplain): "open fields are partly flooded in the winter and attract foraging wigeon and Canada geese".
  - Lhá:lt Harrison Chehalis WMA, near Harrison Mills: "critical wintering, migration and breeding habitats" for aquatic birds and others.
  - Serpentine WMA: on "a major regional flyway for migratory waterfowl moving between Boundary Bay and the agricultural lands".
- Thompson:
  - Tranquille WMA (head of Kamloops Lake): "staging and resting habitat for the spring and fall migration of Canada Goose, swans"; mallard, goldeneye and wood duck nest there (90%). Synopsis Map C17: No Shooting (85%, jumbled map caption).
  - South Thompson River IBA, Little Shuswap Lake to Kamloops Lake (about 70 km): "calm waters ... excellent foraging habitat for wintering waterfowl"; 100 to 400 trumpeter swans and up to 600 tundra swans in winter, https://www.ibacanada.org/mobile/site.jsp?siteID=BC176 (80%). Swans are not game in BC (no season in the synopsis tables, 90%).
- Okanagan:
  - Vaseux Lake IBA, https://www.ibacanada.org/mobile/site.jsp?siteID=BC262 : "resting and overwintering waterfowl, with both Trumpeter and Tundra Swans wintering on the lake"; the lake is a federal Migratory Bird Sanctuary; parcels form the Vaseux Bighorn National Wildlife Area (85%).
  - Okanagan Basin Study, Chapter 9 (Okanagan Basin Water Board, 1970s text), https://www.obwb.ca/newsite/wp-content/uploads/2013/06/68.pdf : North Arm of Okanagan Lake near Vernon hosts "large numbers of migrating and wintering waterfowl"; Vaseux Lake weed mats attract "large numbers of coots and other waterfowl during the fall months"; "The oxbows between Oliver and Osoyoos Lake and the marshes at the north end of Osoyoos Lake provide habitat for waterfowl" (65%, old).
- Weak spots: no source with month by month duck numbers for the Thompson or Okanagan. Months in the data file are built from DU timing plus freeze up (55 to 65%).

### Grouse
- Ruffed grouse: resident.
  - BC Breeding Bird Atlas (Chutter 2015), https://www.birdatlas.bc.ca/accounts/speciesaccount.jsp?sp=RUGR&lang=en : highest abundance 750 to 1,250 m; recorded 5 to 1,697 m; core on central interior plateaus (85%).
  - Vermont Fish and Wildlife, https://www.vtfishandwildlife.com/learn-more/vermont-critters/birds/ruffed-grouse : "non-migratory"; annual home range 6 to 40 acres (2.4 to 16 ha); in fall young birds disperse, older birds stay (70%, US source).
  - Connecticut DEEP fact sheet: "non-migratory, spending their entire life within a small area"; dispersal "largely exhibited by young birds" (70%).
  - Maine IFW: juvenile females can exceed 100 acres (40 ha) in winter (70%).
- Dusky grouse (interior "blue grouse"): elevational migrant, upslope in fall.
  - BC Atlas (Starzomski 2015), https://www.birdatlas.bc.ca/accounts/speciesaccount.jsp?sp=DUGR&lang=en : breeds mainly in IDF and ESSF; "many observations in the 1,500-2,250 m range, although breeding from 500-1,250 m is not uncommon" (85%).
  - Audubon, https://www.audubon.org/field-guide/bird/dusky-grouse : "Most birds move in autumn from fairly open breeding areas to dense coniferous forest. In most parts of range, this involves moving uphill"; "Maximum known travel is about 30 miles" (about 48 km); winter food conifer needles (70%).
  - Colorado Parks and Wildlife, https://cpw.state.co.us/species/dusky-grouse : winter in the high country in Douglas fir and lodgepole pine; "In late March or early April, dusky grouse leave the high country and move to their breeding grounds"; males leave breeding areas by mid July for the highest forest with a huckleberry understory; snow pushes them to wintering grounds (70%).
  - NPS Rocky Mountain NP, https://www.nps.gov/romo/learn/nature/dusky-grouse.htm : "Early fall ... migrate short distances to a higher elevation in a dense forest" (70%).
- Sooty grouse (coast, area B): Birdweb (Seattle Audubon), https://www.birdweb.org/birdweb/bird/sooty_grouse : late summer and early autumn move to dense conifers "at higher elevations"; leave breeding grounds by October, back by early April (65%). Alaska source says winter range slightly lower: direction varies (55%).
- Spruce grouse: resident, short moves.
  - BC Atlas (Chutter 2015): conifer specialist, needles of pine or spruce; known to 2,500 m; recorded 293 to 1,738 m (85%).
  - Herzog and Keppie 1980, The Condor 82: 366 to 372: "movements of up to 9.5 km (5.9 mi) between discrete summer and winter range"; Keppie later recorded one move of 25 km (cited in the Vermont Spruce Grouse Recovery Plan, https://www.vtfishandwildlife.com/sites/fishandwildlife/files/documents/Learn%20More/Library/REPORTS%20AND%20DOCUMENTS/NONGAME%20AND%20NATURAL%20HERITAGE/RECOVERY%20PLANS/Vermont%20Spruce%20Grouse%20Recovery%20Plan.pdf, 70%). Original paper not opened.

### California quail and chukar (resident)
- California quail:
  - BC Atlas (Starzomski 2015), https://www.birdatlas.bc.ca/accounts/speciesaccount.jsp?sp=CAQU&lang=en : two main areas, southeast Vancouver Island and "the Okanagan and Kettle valleys"; "apparent loss of the Fraser Valley population"; prefers PP and BG zones in the interior; "Breeding populations are generally found below 750 m, and most common from 250-500 m, although some may breed as high as 1,250 m"; "common inhabitant of mixed agricultural and suburban areas" with cover and supplementary feeding; also riparian, grassland, open woodland (85%).
  - CDFW (California Department of Fish and Wildlife) life history, https://nrm.dfg.ca.gov/FileHandler.ashx?DocumentID=1707 : "Not migratory"; "Winter movements usually encompass twice the area used in summer"; "Covey may disperse in spring up to 8 km"; winter home range of 4 coveys averaged 10.5 ha (6.9 to 18 ha); "Seldom ventures more than 15-30 m (50-100 ft) from cover"; needs brush, openings and water (75%, California data).
  - iNFOnews, 9 October 2015, https://infotel.ca/newsitem/everything-you-need-to-know-about-quail-in-the-southern-interior/it22857 : "They do not migrate in the winter, but instead form even larger coveys, sometimes with hundreds of birds"; city flocks "are off limits" (60%, news).
  - BC Outdoors, Harris, 4 October 2021, https://www.bcoutdoorsmagazine.com/an-attractive-sporty-california-import/ : "comfortable near people and find food and cover in farmland and suburban areas"; coveys "of two dozen or more"; BC harvest fell from about 4,600 in the 1970s to under 1,000 a year; "expanding urban development has eroded the birds' habitat, and the accompanying hunting restrictions limit hunter opportunities" (65%).
- Chukar:
  - BC Atlas: centre is the Okanagan and Thompson Nicola, "nowhere common"; mostly 250 to 500 m, some to 1,250 m; rocky cliffs, mountainsides, bluebunch wheatgrass, sagebrush, rabbitbrush (85%).
  - Demarchi 1962 thesis, Wildlife Afield 14(2) 2017, http://www.wildlifebc.org/pdfs/WA%2014_2ChukarWeb.pdf : in 1962 "an almost continuous population ... from Lytton to Kamloops"; 195 to 610 m; winter flocks break up late February, pairs by mid March, coveys form from mid June; "Prolonged cold temperatures plus deep snow ... probably constitute the chief inimical factor"; daily water trips at cool hours (80%).
  - FEIS (USDA Fire Effects Information System), Sullivan 1994, https://www.fs.usda.gov/database/feis/animals/bird/alch/all.html : "Chukars exhibit altitudinal migration, moving from higher elevations to lower terrain during heavy snows", may move onto south facing slopes; coveys usually about 20 (75%).
- Pheasant: BC Atlas, Georgia Depression and Okanagan Valley, chiefly below 500 m, gone along the Thompson River (85%). Resident (my reading of atlas plus no migration noted, 75%).
- Wild turkey: BC Atlas (Chutter 2015), expanded "west to the edge of the south Okanagan Valley"; open seasons in the Kootenay and Okanagan (85%). Synopsis page 68: Region 8 turkey in MUs 8-1, 8-9, 8-10, 8-12 to 8-15, 8-22 to 8-26, bearded 15 April to 15 May, any turkey 1 to 15 October, aggregate 2, one per season (99%).

## 2. BEC zones and elevations (southern BC)
Meidinger and Pojar 1991, Ecosystems of BC, BC Ministry of Forests Special Report Series 6, chapters at https://www.for.gov.bc.ca/hfd/pubs/docs/srs/Srs06/chapN.pdf, checked 2026-10-03 (90%):
- BG (Bunchgrass): valley bottoms up to about 700 to 1,000 m; Okanagan to Summerland, Similkameen near Keremeos, Thompson from Spences Bridge to Kamloops, Nicola (chapter 8).
- PP (Ponderosa Pine): 335 to 900 m (chapter 9).
- IDF (Interior Douglas fir): lowest 350 to 600 m, upper 900 to 1,450 m (chapter 10).
- ICH (Interior Cedar Hemlock): 400 to 1,500 m, southeast BC (chapter 11).
- MS (Montane Spruce): 1,100 to 1,500 m wetter, 1,250 to 1,700 m drier (chapter 12).
- ESSF (Engelmann Spruce Subalpine Fir): 1,200 to 2,100 m southwest, 1,500 to 2,300 m southeast (chapter 15).
- Alpine (now IMA, Interior Mountain heather Alpine): above about 2,250 m southeast, 1,650 m southwest (chapter 18, "Alpine Tundra").
- CWH (Coastal Western Hemlock): sea level to 900 m windward, 1,050 m leeward (chapter 6). MH (Mountain Hemlock) 900 to 1,800 m in the south (chapter 7). MH is not in the map's zone list; the data file uses CWH plus ESSF or IMA for coast highs and says so.

## 5. Quail access, season and closures
- Where: Okanagan and Kettle valleys; below 750 m, most at 250 to 500 m; PP and BG zones; farm, orchard and suburban edges with cover; riparian brush (BC Atlas, 85%).
- Cover: brush and trees for feeding, escape, travel and roosting; seldom more than 15 to 30 m from cover; needs water in hot weather (CDFW, 75%).
- Why mostly private:
  - Quail favour farmland and suburbs (BC Atlas 85%; BC Outdoors 65%).
  - South Okanagan grasslands: "More than 40% of remaining grasslands are privately owned, 10% are within Indian Reserves" (SOSCP field guide, https://soscp.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/08/Grassland-species-at-risk.pdf, 75%). The same sentence gives a Crown share of "less than 0.5%", which looks wrong for the wider valley; not used (VERIFY).
  - SOSCP: 70% of grassland habitat gone, 90% of Okanagan River riparian habitat eliminated, 85% of low elevation wetlands gone; "a large percentage of remaining valuable habitats occur on private land" (https://soscp.org/about-soscp/why-is-stewardship-important/, 75%).
  - Valley bottom quail habitat = towns, orchards, vineyards, reserves. My reading: most huntable coveys sit on private or reserve land (75%).
- How hunters get access:
  - Synopsis page 9: "A person is not permitted to hunt on cultivated land or on Crown land which is subject to a grazing lease while the land is occupied by livestock, without the consent of the owner, lessee, or occupant of the land. Note: leased land is subject to the Trespass Act, regardless of whether livestock are present." (99%)
  - Synopsis page 9: "Indian Reserves are private land. Permission must be obtained from the local Indian business office" (99%).
  - Crown parcels: find with ParcelMap BC or iMapBC (app phase2/08 and phase6/13); the Spot Finder candidate layer filters Crown land under 700 m near farmland (SPOTS.md).
  - Forum advice: start early, door knock, "hopefully you can find some friendly farmers" (search preview, 50%).
- Season and limits (synopsis page 68, 2 October 2026 edition, 99%): QUAIL, MUs 8-1 to 8-15 and 8-21 to 8-26, 1 October to 30 November, 10 daily, 30 possession. Chukar same MUs and dates, 5 (15). Pheasant cocks 2 (6). No quail rows in the Region 2 or Region 3 tables (my reading, 90%).
- Closures: Vaseux Migratory Bird Sanctuary and National Wildlife Areas, no hunting (page 67, 99%); NWAs and sanctuaries, "Blue signs depicting a loon mark the boundaries" (page 9, 99%); Osoyoos Oxbows closed 1 March to 30 September (page 69, 99%); Kaleden and Okanagan Falls No Shooting Areas and Skaha Lake shot only area (Map J10, page 69, 99%). Town bylaws: see phase6/13.

## 6. Camping rules (official)
- Crown land outside rec sites: Land Use Policy, Permission (effective 16 May 2014, amended 1 December 2023), section 2, https://www2.gov.bc.ca/assets/gov/farming-natural-resources-and-industry/natural-resource-use/land-water-use/crown-land/permissions.pdf (99%):
  - "Any person may camp on Crown land for up to 14 consecutive calendar days."
  - The count resets only if "the person and their vehicle and equipment ... are not present on the site for a period of at least 72 consecutive hours."
  - Conditions: no permanent structures; campfires per the campfire guide; remove all garbage; respect other users.
  - The policy itself is the "lawful authority": no application and no fee (my reading, 85%).
- Recreation sites: Forest Recreation Regulation, B.C. Reg. 16/2004, https://www.bclaws.gov.bc.ca/civix/document/id/complete/statreg/16_2004 (99%):
  - Section 13: "A person must not camp at a recreation site for a period exceeding 14 consecutive days", same 72 hour reset.
  - Section 11: no trap, firearm, bow or crossbow "on or into the developed portion"; developed portion = structures plus a 100 m buffer (definitions).
  - Section 10: no game offal, entrails, hides or bones in the developed portion.
  - Section 14: do not remove firewood from a site.
  - Section 21: a camping fee covers 24 hours from 12 noon.
- RSTBC (Recreation Sites and Trails BC) pages, gov.bc.ca:
  - Fees page (updated 3 February 2026): fees at some sites, "collected by operators (cash only)"; "Most recreation site campsites are available on a first-come, first-served basis"; a few fee sites take reservations; one vehicle per party, extra vehicles pay 50% of the daily fee (99%).
  - Rules page (updated 18 July 2025): 14 consecutive days; do not hold sites or leave property (removed after 24 hours); campfires 0.5 m by 0.5 m in rings, 8 litres of water and hand tools ready, never unattended; quiet 11 pm to 7 am; 20 km/h; no firearms discharge, no game entrails on site; report fires at *5555 or 1 800 663 5555 (99%).
- Fire bans: BC Wildfire Service, https://www2.gov.bc.ca/gov/content/safety/wildfire-status/prevention/fire-bans-and-restrictions (updated 2 October 2026):
  - Kamloops Fire Centre covers Wells Gray to the US border including the South Okanagan (areas A and C); Coastal Fire Centre covers the Lower Mainland (area B) (99%).
  - Status read 2026-10-03: Category 1 campfires "Permitted" in Kamloops (page updated 2 October 2026) and Coastal (updated 30 September 2026), no area restrictions (99% for that date only; changes fast).
  - Category 1 campfire: no larger than 0.5 m high by 0.5 m wide, fuel break, a person watching with a hand tool or 8 litres of water (open burning page, updated 1 September 2026, 99%).
  - Fines: ticket $1,150, administrative penalty $10,000, court up to $100,000 and 1 year; causing a wildfire up to $1 million and 3 years plus costs (99%).
  - Where to check: BC Wildfire Service map https://wildfiresituation.nrs.gov.bc.ca/map, the fire centre pages, the park page, BC Parks advisories, RSTBC alerts and closures (99%).
- WMAs: B.C. Reg. 24/2015 Wildlife Management Area Use and Access Regulation, Schedules 1 and 2: no camping and no campfires in Bert Brink, Boundary Bay, Lhá:lt Harrison Chehalis, Pitt Addington Marsh and Serpentine WMAs, among others (99%). Dogs must be leashed in Schedule 4 WMAs unless lawfully hunting (99%).
- Parks: hunting and camping rules are per park page (synopsis page 9, 99%). Not researched park by park for camping (VERIFY).
